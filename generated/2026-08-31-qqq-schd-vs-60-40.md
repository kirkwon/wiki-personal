# QQQ/SCHD vs 60/40 — chain of thought (generated/quarantined)

## Conversation context
User considering replacing a 60/40 equities/bonds portfolio with a QQQ/SCHD

1. Does QQQ/SCHD provide a hedging effect relative to 60/40?
2. Are bonds worth the risk premium when HYS or 0-3mo govt securities offer
   competitive yield with near-zero duration?

---

## Question 1 — Does QQQ/SCHD hedge like a 60/40?

### 60/40 bond sleeve — what it hedges with
- Bond sleeve (the 40%) is the hedge in a 60/40
- Textbook mechanism: flight-to-quality + Fed cuts during equity selloffs → bonds
  rally → negative correlation to equities → downside protection + vol dampener
- Imperfect: 2022 was the counterexample (both bonds and stocks fell together)

### Post-2010 regime shift — bond hedge reliability collapsed
- Historical negative stock/bond correlation was largely a disinflationary/growth-scare
  regime artifact (slowdown → Fed cuts → bonds rally)
- Post-2010, dominant risk factor shifted toward inflation and rate volatility
- Inflation-shock regime: bonds sell off on rate increases, stocks sell off on
  discounting + margin pressure → both down together
- 2022 concrete example: S&P 500 -19.4%, Bloomberg US Agg -13.0% — both down,
  hedge failed exactly when wanted
- Rolling 1yr stock/bond correlation: swung from structurally negative pre-2000
  (often -0.4 to -0.6) to near-zero or positive in 2010s/2020s
- Verdict: bond hedge is now regime-dependent, not structurally reliable

### QQQ/SCHD — what they actually diversify with
- Both 100% equity
- QQQ: growth/momentum/tech-concentrated (~40%+ info tech, mega-cap growth heavy)
- SCHD: dividend/value screen — tilts financials, industrials, consumer staples,
  healthcare, defensive sectors
- Long-run correlation QQQ↔SCHD ~0.75–0.85 — lower than two random equity indexes,
  but solidly positive (no negative correlation to equities)
- Factor diversification is real but modest — addresses factor concentration risk,
  not equity-beta risk
- Yields differ: SCHD ~3–3.5% (equity dividend), QQQ ~0.5% — neither is a fixed
  coupon that goes up when stocks crash

### User rebuttal (the key pivot)
- "After 2010 bonds are almost as correlated to stocks — they just don't return
  as much"
- Implication: if bonds aren't reliably negatively correlated post-2010 anyway,
  then the "loss of the bond hedge" argument for QQQ/SCHD weakens — the bond
  component wasn't reliably hedging to begin with

### Synthesis — relative framing
- Real comparison: relative to a 60/40 where the bond sleeve is itself an imperfect
  post-2010 hedge, does QQQ/SCHD leave you meaningfully more exposed or meaningfully
  better-off?
- On exposure: you give up whatever (imperfect) diversification the bond sleeve still
  provides, concentrate equity beta in two US equity factors
- On upside/honesty: you've tilted equity toward quality/dividend (SCHD) and growth
  (QQQ), removed duration risk, built a portfolio more honest about being 100% equity
- Verdict: if 60/40 was wanted for crash protection, post-2010 data says that
  protection was already shaky — so QQQ/SCHD isn't really giving up much that was
  reliable. If 60/40 was wanted for lower volatility / smoother ride, 60/40 still
  generally delivers that vs 100% equity — QQQ/SCHD won't match it.

### Bottom line — Q1
- QQQ/SCHD does NOT hedge like 60/40 — it's 100% equity, no negative correlation
  to equities
- But if the bond component wasn't reliably hedging post-2010 anyway, the "you lost
  the hedge" argument is weaker than the textbook framing implies
- Open: what was the 60/40 actually wanted for? (crash protection → already shaky;
  vol reduction → 60/40 still wins)

## Question 2 — Bonds vs HYS / 0-3mo govt securities

### What the comparison actually is
- Not "bonds vs cash" — it's **duration risk for a term premium** vs **zero-duration
  cash equivalents at a competitive yield**
- HYS / 0-3mo T-bills / MMF: zero or near-zero duration, yield rolls with Fed policy,
  reinvestment risk only (rates drop → reinvest lower), no price risk
- Intermediate/long bonds: taking rate sensitivity (duration) in exchange for a term
  premium that post-2010 has been surprisingly thin or intermittently negative

### Term premium reality — post-2010
- Historically: term premium positive and reliable enough to justify duration risk
- Post-2010, structurally post-2022 yield-curve dynamics: term premium thin, sometimes
  negative, regime-dependent
- 2022-2024: 10yr-3mo spread spent significant time inverted — short end yielded more
  than long end → literally paid more to take zero duration
- Regime inverts old logic: reaching for yield with duration pays you less AND takes
  more risk — cash equivalents win on both yield and risk in that regime

### The hedging angle (tie-back to Q1)
- If holding bonds as equity hedge: does the hedging value justify the term-premium
  drag / duration risk?
- Post-2010: hedging value unreliable (regime-dependent, failed 2022)
- If in a regime where bonds aren't reliably hedging AND short end pays as much or
  more than long end → case for intermediate/long bonds as "hold for term premium +
  hedge" is weak — taking duration risk for thin/negative premium and unreliable hedge

### Where bonds still make sense
1. Liability matching — known future cash need, lock in yield for that specific horizon.
   Term premium isn't the point; matching is.
2. Diversification into a different risk factor — bonds still SOMETIMES negatively
   correlated to equities (growth-scare regimes), occasional diversification has some
   value even if not reliable. "Sometimes helpful" not "reliable hedge."

### Honest framing
- "Should I hold bonds for the return premium over HYS/T-bills?" → not clearly, often no.
  Term premium thin-to-negative, short end frequently paid more, duration risk must be
  justified by liability need or acceptance of occasional (unreliable) diversification.
- "Should I hold bonds as a hedge?" → same as Q1: hedge unreliable post-2010; want
  negative-beta exposure? Long-only bonds don't reliably deliver it anymore.

### Implication for the QQQ/SCHD substitution
- If bonds aren't reliably hedging AND aren't clearly paying a premium over cash →
  a 60/40 where the 40% is bonds carries two imperfect instruments (dubious hedge +
  thin term premium)
- Swapping the 40% to cash equivalents (HYS/short T-bills): zero duration, competitive
  yield, removes rate risk → portfolio becomes 60% equity (QQQ/SCHD or total market) +
  cash sleeve — different animal than a 60/40
