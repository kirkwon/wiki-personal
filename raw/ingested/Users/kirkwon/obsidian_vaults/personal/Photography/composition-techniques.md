## Overview

Composition is how you arrange elements in your frame. Good composition makes your photos more visually appealing, guides the viewer's eye, and creates emotional impact.

## The Rule of Thirds

### What It Is

Divide your frame into a 3x3 grid (9 equal parts). Place important elements at the intersections of the grid lines, not in the center.

### Why It Works

Creates balance and natural tension, mimics how humans naturally view scenes.

### How to Apply

1. **Enable grid** on your camera (most cameras have this feature)
2. **Place subject at intersections** (4 points where lines cross)
3. **Align horizon with grid lines** (top or bottom third, not middle)

### Examples

| Scene | Placement |
|-------|-----------|
| Portrait with horizon | Subject at intersection, horizon at top or bottom third |
| Landscape | Horizon at top or bottom third, interesting element at intersection |
| Street scene | Main subject at intersection, leading lines to that point |

### When to Break the Rule

- **Center composition**: Symmetry, strong central subject
- **Breaking for effect**: Tension, imbalance

## Leading Lines

### What They Are

Lines in your image that guide the viewer's eye to your main subject.

### Types of Leading Lines

| Line Type | Example | Effect |
|------------|----------|--------|
| **Straight** | Roads, fences, buildings | Direct, strong |
| **Curved** | Rivers, paths, coastlines | Soft, flowing |
| **Diagonal** | Stairs, shadows | Dynamic, energy |
| **Converging** | Railroad tracks, roads | Perspective, depth |

### How to Use

1. **Find lines** in your scene (roads, fences, shadows, architecture)
2. **Position yourself** so lines lead to your subject
3. **Frame the shot** so lines start at edge, go to subject
4. **Multiple lines** can create depth and interest

### Examples

- **Road to sunset**: Road edges frame sun in distance
- **Building shadows**: Shadows lead to subject
- **Railroad tracks**: Converging lines to distant subject

## Framing

### What It Is

Using elements in your scene to create a "frame" around your main subject.

### Types of Frames

| Frame Type | Example | Effect |
|------------|----------|--------|
| **Natural** | Trees, arches, doorways | Adds depth, context |
| **Architectural** | Windows, door frames, arches | Adds structure |
| **Environmental** | Leaves, branches, rocks | Adds organic feel |
| **Light** | Silhouettes, light beams | Adds drama |

### How to Use

1. **Find natural frames** (doorways, windows, trees)
2. **Position subject** behind or inside frame
3. **Frame doesn't need to be complete**: Partial framing works
4. **Depth of field**: Blurred foreground frame, sharp subject

### Examples

- **Portrait through doorway**: Subject framed by door
- **Person behind tree branches**: Branches frame person
- **Cityscape through tunnel**: Tunnel frames city view

## Symmetry and Patterns

### Symmetry

**What it is:** Mirrored elements, balance.

**Types:**
- **Vertical symmetry**: Reflection in water, symmetrical architecture
- **Horizontal symmetry**: Balanced top and bottom
- **Radial symmetry**: From center point (circles, spirals)

**How to use:**
1. **Find symmetrical scenes**: Architecture, reflections
2. **Center the frame**: Place symmetry line in center
3. **Perfect alignment**: Use grid, level camera carefully

**Examples:**
- **Reflection in water**: Mirrored landscape
- **Symmetrical building**: Facade symmetrical
- **Face**: Natural symmetry

### Patterns

**What it is:** Repeating elements.

**How to use:**
1. **Find repeating elements**: Windows, bricks, leaves, textures
2. **Fill the frame**: Make pattern the subject
3. **Break the pattern**: One element different creates interest

**Examples:**
- **Windows on building**: Repeating pattern
- **Brick wall**: Textural pattern
- **Row of trees**: Natural pattern

## Depth and Layers

### Creating Depth

**Why it matters:** 3D images in 2D medium.

**Techniques:**

| Technique | How | Example |
|-----------|-----|---------|
| **Foreground element** | Place object close to camera | Tree branch in front of landscape |
| **Background element** | Include distant elements | Mountains in background |
---
| **Leading lines** | Lines create depth | Road receding into distance |
| **Aerial perspective** | Distant objects less sharp, more blue | Mountains appear blue and hazy |
| **Layers** | Multiple planes (fore, middle, back) | Tree → field → mountains |

### Layering

**Three basic layers:**
1. **Foreground**: Closest to camera, creates depth
2. **Middle ground**: Main subject
3. **Background**: Sets context, adds depth

## Negative Space

### What It Is

Empty space around your main subject.

### Why Use It

- **Emphasizes subject**: Subject stands out
- **Creates breathing room**: Uncluttered, peaceful
- **Adds impact**: Minimalist, powerful
- **Directional**: Negative space can suggest movement

### How to Use

1. **Find minimalist scenes**: Sky, water, walls
2. **Place subject in one part of frame**
3. **Leave rest empty**: Large areas of negative space
4. **Balance**: Subject vs. negative space

### Examples

- **Person in vast sky**: Subject small, sky dominates
- **Small boat in ocean**: Boat small, water dominates
- **Solitary tree in field**: Tree subject, empty field

## Golden Ratio

### What It Is

Mathematical ratio (1:1.618) found in nature and art. More complex version of rule of thirds.

### How to Apply

- **Spiral (Fibonacci)**: Subject on spiral curve
- **Golden triangle**: Subject at intersection of diagonal lines
- **Golden ratio grid**: Similar to rule of thirds but different proportions

### When to Use

- **Portraits**: Place eye on golden ratio point
- **Landscapes**: Horizon at golden ratio line
- **Abstract**: Create mathematical harmony

## Color Theory in Photography

### Color Harmony

| Harmony | Colors | Effect |
|---------|--------|--------|
| **Monochromatic** | Shades of one color | Unified, calm |
| **Analogous** | Adjacent on color wheel | Harmonious, natural |
| **Complementary** | Opposite on color wheel | High contrast, energetic |
| **Split complementary** | One color + two adjacent to opposite | Balanced contrast |
| **Triadic** | Three equally spaced colors | Vibrant, balanced |

### Color Temperature

- **Warm colors**: Red, orange, yellow (sunset, fire, sunrise)
- **Cool colors**: Blue, green, purple (water, shade, twilight)
- **Warm vs. cool contrast**: Creates visual interest

## Perspective

### Camera Position

**Change perspective to create interest:**

| Position | Effect | Example |
|----------|--------|---------|
| **Eye level** | Natural, documentary | Street photography |
| **Low angle** | Powerful, grand | Architecture, portraits |
| **High angle** | Diminished, overview | Landscapes, crowds |
| **Bird's eye** | Abstract, graphic | Cityscapes, patterns |
| **Worm's eye** | Dramatic, towering | Buildings, trees |

## Minimalism

### What It Is

Simplifying composition to essential elements.

### Techniques

1. **Remove distractions**: Keep only what's necessary
2. **Simple backgrounds**: Plain walls, sky, water
3. **One main subject**: Clear focal point
4. **Negative space**: Lots of empty space
5. **Clean lines**: Straight, simple shapes

### Examples

- **Person against white wall**: Minimal portrait
- **Single leaf on table**: Simple still life
- **Horizon line**: Minimal landscape

## Breaking the Rules

### When to Break

- **For effect**: Tension, imbalance, surprise
- **Creative expression**: Your vision
- **Subject demands it**: Sometimes subject better in center

### Examples of Breaking Rules

- **Center composition**: For symmetry or dramatic effect
- **Cluttered background**: For environmental portraits
- **No leading lines**: For flat, graphic images
- **Rule-breaking for storytelling**: When it serves the narrative

## Common Composition Mistakes

| Mistake | How to Fix |
|---------|------------|
| Subject in center | Move to rule of thirds intersection |
| Cluttered background | Move to cleaner background, use wider aperture |
| No focal point | Have one clear subject, eliminate distractions |
| Headroom too much/little | Crop appropriately |
| Crooked horizon | Use grid, level camera |
| Subject too small | Get closer or use telephoto lens |

## Practice Exercises

### Daily Practice
1. **Rule of thirds**: Practice placing subject at intersections
2. **Leading lines**: Find and use leading lines in every shot
3. **Framing**: Find frames (natural or architectural) for your shots

### Weekly Practice
1. **One technique per week**: Master rule of thirds, then leading lines, etc.
2. **Study master photographers**: Analyze their composition
3. **Practice breaking rules**: Understand rules before breaking them
4. **Camera angles**: Shoot same scene from different perspectives

## Analyzing Composition

### When viewing photos

1. **Identify main subject**: What is the photo about?
2. **Trace eye movement**: How do you look at the photo?
3. **Identify techniques used**: Rule of thirds, leading lines, etc.
4. **Notice balance**: How is the photo balanced or unbalanced?
5. **Consider color**: How does color contribute?

### When creating photos

1. **Identify subject**: What's the main subject?
2. **Choose technique**: Which composition technique works?
3. **Frame carefully**: Check edges for distractions
4. **Review in camera**: Zoom in, check composition
5. **Take multiple shots**: Vary composition, choose best
