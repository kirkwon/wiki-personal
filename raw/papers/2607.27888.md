Abstract

Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) is central to improving long-CoT reasoning in large language models. Critic-free methods such as GRPO convert response-level rewards into advantages and uniformly broadcast them across tokens, overlooking their unequal contributions to the final outcome. On-policy self-distillation (OPSD) instead provides dense distributional supervision by minimizing the forward KL divergence between an unprivileged policy and a privileged self-teacher, implicitly assuming that the resulting likelihood shifts encode reliable answer-aligned information. We test this premise by fixing each sampled trajectory and re-scoring it under two opposing outcome conditions, one asserting correctness and the other incorrectness. Most affected tokens shift in the same direction under both conditions, with few sign reversals and substantial overlap in the induced optimization signals. Large shifts also concentrate on highly substitutable surface-form tokens, whereas tokens carrying problem-specific reasoning content are less sensitive. These findings show that privileged shifts fail to provide reliable answer-aligned directions, while their magnitudes primarily reflect counterfactual sensitivity rather than token-level learning value. Based on these observations, we propose Counterfactual Sensitivity Credit Reallocation (CSCR), a simple extension of GRPO that reduces credit for highly sensitive tokens and renormalizes token-level advantages to preserve both the original credit budget and verifier-determined direction. On long-CoT mathematical reasoning benchmarks, CSCR consistently outperforms GRPO baseline with the same number of policy updates. Targeted ablations further corroborate our diagnosis: privilege-induced directions are unreliable, moderate downweighting is most effective, and stronger modulation destabilizes optimization.

1Introduction

Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) has emerged as a powerful post-training paradigm for improving large language models in domains such as mathematics and code, where final outcomes can be evaluated automatically and at scale Shao et al. (2024); Lambert et al. (2024); Guo et al. (2025); Yu et al. (2026); Hu et al. (2026); Wen et al. (2025). Critic-free methods such as Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) and its recent variants estimate relative advantages from groups of sampled responses and uniformly broadcast each sequence-level advantage across all generated tokens, avoiding learned value models and costly process-level annotation Shao et al. (2024); Liu et al. (2025); Yu et al. (2026). This simplicity has enabled scalable long-CoT training, but also creates a fundamental mismatch between outcome-level evaluation and token-level optimization: within a response spanning thousands of tokens, decisive reasoning steps, exploratory branches, and largely prefix-determined continuations receive identical credit despite contributing differently to the final outcome Kazemnejad et al. (2024); Parthasarathi et al. (2025); Tan et al. (2025). Process-supervision approaches seek to address this mismatch by assigning denser intermediate feedback, but typically rely on step-level annotations, auxiliary reward models, or additional rollouts Lightman et al. (2024); Luo et al. (2024). Developing reliable and scalable token-level credit signals while retaining the simplicity of outcome-supervised RLVR therefore remains a central challenge for long-CoT reasoning.

On-policy distillation (OPD) provides dense token-level distributional supervision by querying a teacher on student-generated trajectories under the same prefixes, thereby reducing the train–inference distribution mismatch inherent in off-policy distillation Gu et al. (2024); Agarwal et al. (2024); Ko et al. (2024); Jin et al. (2026). However, standard OPD is not naturally aligned with RLVR, since teacher likelihoods reflect the teacher’s own distribution rather than verifier correctness and may therefore reinforce incorrect trajectories or suppress valid student solutions Zheng et al. (2026). Its effectiveness also depends strongly on teacher–student compatibility: even a stronger teacher may provide ineffective supervision when its reasoning patterns diverge from those of the student Li et al. (2026b). Moreover, evaluating a separate teacher over every student-generated trajectory incurs substantial computational and memory overhead, especially for long-form reasoning Li et al. (2026b); Yang et al. (2026c). These limitations have motivated on-policy self-distillation (OPSD), where the policy itself serves as a privileged teacher conditioned on verified answers, reference solutions, or outcome feedback, and transfers its conditional distribution to the unprivileged branch through KL optimization Zhao et al. (2026a); Penaloza et al. (2026). Recent studies have extended this paradigm through feedback-conditioned self-teaching Hübotter et al. (2026), hybrid RL–distillation objectives and adaptive routing Li et al. (2026a), verifier-aligned or outcome-guided calibration of the self-teacher Yang et al. (2026a, b), and contrastive, reflective, or error-focused supervision Pan et al. (2026); Zhao et al. (2026b). These approaches share a common premise: privileged distributional changes contain answer-relevant token-level information that can be extracted to guide policy optimization.

To examine this premise, we construct two counterfactual privileged conditions that semantically mirror the sequence-level credit assignment of GRPO. In a mixed-reward GRPO group, a correct final answer induces positive credit uniformly broadcast across the entire trajectory, whereas an incorrect answer induces uniformly negative credit. OPSD assumes that conditioning the policy on privileged information 
𝑐

We test this assumption using 400 on-policy trajectories, comprising four responses for each of 100 questions from DAPO-17K Yu et al. (2026). We fix each trajectory and all token prefixes and re-score them using the polarized positive and negative prompts in Table 1. On these trajectories, we analyze shift direction, overlap in forward-KL optimization signals, and the token types associated with large shifts. 3Method

Building on the preceding analysis, CSCR uses counterfactual sensitivity to reallocate GRPO credit across tokens. Tokens with higher sensitivity receive smaller token-level weights, while the verifier-derived sequence advantage continues to determine the optimization direction. The resulting weights are renormalized to preserve the credit budget.

4.1Experimental Setup
Datasets.

We use DAPO-17K Yu et al. (2026) as the training dataset, which contains challenging mathematical reasoning problems designed for long-CoT reinforcement learning. We evaluate all methods on five held-out competition-level benchmarks: AMC23 MAA and Math-AI (2023), AIME24 Zhang and Math-AI (2024), AIME25 Zhang and Math-AI (2025), AIME26 Zhang and Math-AI (2026), and SMT-2025 Dekoninck et al. (2026). These benchmarks cover varying levels of mathematical difficulty and enable a more comprehensive evaluation of overall reasoning performance and robustness on challenging problems.

Baselines.

We compare CSCR with GRPO Shao et al. (2024) and five self-distillation methods. OPSD Zhao et al. (2026a) distills the token distributions of a privileged self-teacher along on-policy rollouts. SDPO Hübotter et al. (2026) converts feedback-conditioned self-teacher predictions into dense token-level supervision. SRPO Li et al. (2026a) routes correct rollouts to GRPO and incorrect rollouts to SDPO, with entropy-aware weighting applied to the distillation branch. RLSD Yang et al. (2026a) uses verifier-derived advantages for update directions and privileged likelihood differences to modulate token-level credit magnitudes. RLCSD Pan et al. (2026) contrasts correct and incorrect student rollouts to isolate task-relevant privileged shifts and uses the contrastive signal for token-level credit modulation.

5Conclusion

We investigate whether privileged likelihood shifts provide reliable token-level supervision for long-CoT RLVR. Across 400 on-policy trajectories, our analysis separates shift direction from magnitude at both sampled-token and full-vocabulary levels under fixed prefixes. Counterfactual re-scoring shows that opposing outcome conditions induce predominantly same-direction shifts and substantially overlapping forward-KL signals, while large shifts concentrate on substitutable surface-form tokens rather than problem-specific reasoning content. We therefore reinterpret shift magnitude as counterfactual sensitivity and propose CSCR, a minimal GRPO extension that preserves verifier-derived direction and trajectory-level credit while reallocating credit away from highly sensitive tokens. CSCR consistently outperforms GRPO and all self-distillation baselines across both model scales and all five benchmarks. Ablations further show that trusting shift signs causes rapid optimization collapse, moderate downweighting yields the strongest and most stable gains, and stronger modulation in either direction destabilizes optimization. These results challenge privileged distributional changes as direct supervision and establish counterfactual sensitivity as a practical basis for reliable token-level credit reallocation.

C.1Alternative Counterfactual Privileged Prompt Constructions

To assess whether our findings depend on the deliberately polarized prompts used in the main text, we construct three additional pairs of counterfactual privileged prompts, summarized in Table C.1. For all constructions, we keep the problem, sampled trajectory, and every token prefix fixed, modifying only the additional privileged context used during likelihood re-scoring. Within each construction, the positive and negative conditions follow the same prompt template and differ only in the privileged information that determines their intended correctness polarity.

The answer-level construction holds the ground-truth answer fixed and counterfactually describes the same answer as either correct or incorrect. It therefore isolates the effect of asserted answer-level correctness while controlling for the answer content itself. The mild outcome-only construction states only whether the sampled trajectory reaches the correct final answer, without revealing the answer, providing a reference solution, identifying a specific error, or explicitly evaluating the quality of the reasoning process. Compared with the polarized prompts used in the main text, this construction removes strongly evaluative language and retains only concise trajectory-level outcome feedback. Finally, the reference-solution construction provides either a correct or an incorrect reasoning process through otherwise identical prompt templates. The correct and incorrect reference solutions are generated by DeepSeek-V4-Pro and subsequently reviewed manually to confirm that they satisfy their designated correctness labels.

Together, these three constructions cover privileged information at the answer, trajectory-outcome, and full-reasoning levels. They allow us to determine whether the observed likelihood-shift patterns persist across substantially different forms and strengths of counterfactual conditioning, rather than arising specifically from the polarized wording adopted in the main experiments.

Sensitive Token Types under Alternative Prompts.

Tables C.3, C.4, and C.5 reveal a highly consistent lexical pattern across all three counterfactual prompt constructions. Under both positive and negative privileged conditions, the large-shift lists are dominated by sentence starters, discourse markers, function words, and other locally substitutable surface-form tokens, including The, But, So, Therefore, Wait, Alternatively, Let, Maybe, and Check. Many of the same token identities also occur frequently in both the positive- and negative-shift tails, showing that shift sign does not isolate distinct classes of outcome-supporting and outcome-opposing tokens. This pattern remains evident under reference-solution conditioning, even though the positive and negative privileged contexts contain complete reasoning processes with opposing correctness polarity.
