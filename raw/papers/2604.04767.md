Title: Exploration on Adaptively Reformulated Instances Enables Learning from Hard Reasoning Problems

URL Source: https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767

Published Time: Tue, 07 Apr 2026 01:34:58 GMT

Markdown Content:

###### Abstract

Reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards (RLVR) has improved the reasoning abilities of large language models (LLMs), yet a fundamental limitation remains: models cannot learn from problems that are too difficult to solve under their current policy, as these yield no meaningful reward signal. This creates a performance ceiling where further training yields diminishing returns. We propose a simple yet effective solution based on task reformulation. We transform challenging open-ended problems into cognitively simpler variants – such as multiple-choice and cloze formats – that preserve the original answer while reducing the effective search space and providing denser learning signals. These reformulations span a spectrum from discriminative to generative tasks, which we exploit to bootstrap learning: models first learn from structured, easier formats, and this knowledge transfers back to improve performance on the original open-ended problems. Building on this insight, we introduce Cog-DRIFT, a framework that automatically constructs reformulated variants and organizes them into an adaptive curriculum based on difficulty. Training progresses from easier to harder formats as the model improves, enabling the model to learn from problems that previously yielded zero signal under standard RL post-training. Cog-DRIFT not only improves on the originally unsolvable hard problems (+10.11% for Qwen and +8.64% for Llama in absolute gains), but also generalizes well to other held-out datasets. Across two models and six challenging reasoning benchmarks, our method consistently outperforms standard GRPO and strong guided-exploration baselines. On average, Cog-DRIFT shows +4.72% (Qwen) and +3.23% (Llama) improvements over the second-best baseline. We further show that Cog-DRIFT improves pass@k at test time, and the curriculum improves sample efficiency. Overall, our results highlight task reformulation and curriculum learning as an effective paradigm for overcoming the exploration barrier in LLM post-training.1 1 1 Code is available at: [https://github.com/dinobby/Cog-DRIFT](https://github.com/dinobby/Cog-DRIFT)

## 1 Introduction

Learning from problems that exceed a learner’s current capacity is inherently difficult. This challenge is not unique to models: in human learning, the _Zone of Proximal Development_(Vygotsky et al., [1978](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib31 "The development of higher psychological processes")) describes how learning is maximized when tasks are neither too easy nor too difficult, but instead lie just beyond the learner’s current capabilities and can be solved with appropriate scaffolding (Wood et al., [1976](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib103 "THE role of tutoring in problem solving"); Berk and Winsler, [1995](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib32 "Scaffolding children’s learning: vygotsky and early childhood education. naeyc research into practice series. volume 7.")). Educational psychology further shows that problem format plays a central role in shaping cognitive load and learning dynamics. Structured formats such as multiple-choice (MCQs) (Kelly, [1916](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib97 "The kansas silent reading tests."); Cantor et al., [2014](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib86 "Multiple-choice tests stabilize access to marginal knowledge")) and fill-in-the-blank (cloze) tasks (Taylor, [1953](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib101 "“Cloze procedure”: a new tool for measuring readability"); Polat, [2020](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib87 "Analysis of multiple-choice versus open-ended questions in language tests according to different cognitive domain levels.")) have been used to reduce cognitive load and scaffold learning (Sweller, [1988](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib85 "Cognitive load during problem solving: effects on learning"); Cantor et al., [2014](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib86 "Multiple-choice tests stabilize access to marginal knowledge"); Gog et al., [2004](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib89 "Process-oriented worked examples: improving transfer performance through enhanced understanding")). Theoretically, constraining the effective search space and transforming generation into recognition reduces cognitive load (Ko, [2010](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib94 "A comparison of reading comprehension tests: multiple-choice vs. open-ended."); Howard et al., [2017](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib93 "The method of educational assessment affects children’s neural processing and performance: behavioural and fmri evidence")). Over time, repeated exposure to such scaffolded tasks supports learning and facilitates transfer to more complex, open-ended problems (Gog et al., [2004](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib89 "Process-oriented worked examples: improving transfer performance through enhanced understanding"); Sweller et al., [1998](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib90 "Cognitive architecture and instructional design")). Taken together, these insights from human learning suggest a compelling analogy for training large language models (LLMs): when a problem is too difficult to learn from directly, reformulating it into structured variants might bring it into the model’s Zone of Proximal Development, enabling learning signals that would otherwise be inaccessible.

This perspective is particularly relevant for Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Reward (RLVR). While RLVR has become a dominant learning paradigm to improve LLM reasoning (Guo et al., [2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib2 "Deepseek-r1: incentivizing reasoning capability in llms via reinforcement learning")), a key limitation remains in commonly used algorithms such as PPO (Schulman et al., [2017](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib15 "Proximal policy optimization algorithms")) and GRPO (Shao et al., [2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib17 "Deepseekmath: pushing the limits of mathematical reasoning in open language models")), which we visualize in [Figure 1](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#S1.F1 "In 1 Introduction ‣ Cog-DRIFT: Exploration on Adaptively Reformulated Instances Enables Learning from Hard Reasoning Problems")(A): when models are trained directly on difficult problems, on-policy RL often fails to obtain any positive reward signal, leading to zero advantages and no meaningful updates. Here, we define “difficult” as pass@64=0, following past work (Jiang et al., [2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib102 "FATE: a formal benchmark series for frontier algebra of multiple difficulty levels")). This failure mode resembles the “out of the Zone of Proximal Development” setting in human learning, where tasks are too difficult for learning to occur. Prior work has attempted to address this issue in two main ways: either by leveraging stronger models to generate expert trajectories for guidance(Qu et al., [2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib6 "Learning to reason on hard problems with privileged on-policy exploration")), or by using the same policy to generate either easier problems or hints to reduce problem difficulty (Sundaram et al., [2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib91 "Teaching models to teach themselves: reasoning at the edge of learnability"); Chen et al., [2025a](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib12 "Nudging the boundaries of llm reasoning"); Li et al., [2025b](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib11 "Questa: expanding reasoning capacity in llms via question augmentation"); Wang et al., [2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib83 "Beyond accuracy: dissecting mathematical reasoning for llms under reinforcement learning")). However, stronger models are not always available, and soliciting expert guidance can be costly, limiting its scalability. Moreover, hard problems often represent the ceiling of capacity, so obtaining solutions from an expert model or human may not be feasible (i.e., even too hard for experts). Self-generated hints can be effective, but are also limited by the model’s overall capabilities, leaving room for improvement (c.f. [Section 2](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#S2 "2 Related Work ‣ Cog-DRIFT: Exploration on Adaptively Reformulated Instances Enables Learning from Hard Reasoning Problems")).

We instead propose task reformulation as a practical solution. By rewriting open-ended questions (OEQ) as multiple-choice (MCQ) or fill-in-the-blank (cloze) tasks, the output space is dramatically constrained and learning signals become far denser. Importantly, these reformulations span a spectrum between discriminative and generative learning tasks. MCQs are more discriminative, turning the task into one of ranking or selection. Cloze tasks are partially generative, requiring the model to produce missing components of the answer. OEQs lie at the most generative extreme. Our approach leverages this spectrum by using discriminative tasks to bootstrap learning: generative tasks teach models to avoid shortcuts (e.g., skipping reasoning steps, outputting the correct answer choice with partial input) (Balepur et al., [2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib98 "Which of these best describes multiple choice evaluation with llms? a) forced b) flawed c) fixable d) all of the above"); Chandak et al., [2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib96 "Answer matching outperforms multiple choice for language model evaluation"); Li et al., [2025a](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib95 "Generative classifiers avoid shortcut solutions")) while discriminative tasks are easier to learn (Geirhos et al., [2020](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib99 "Shortcut learning in deep neural networks")). In [Figure 1](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#S1.F1 "In 1 Introduction ‣ Cog-DRIFT: Exploration on Adaptively Reformulated Instances Enables Learning from Hard Reasoning Problems")(B) and [Section 3](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#S3 "3 Problem Statement ‣ Cog-DRIFT: Exploration on Adaptively Reformulated Instances Enables Learning from Hard Reasoning Problems"), we find that once the model learns on the reformulated tasks, it can transfer back to the original open-ended setting. However, we find that naively using a single reformulation is insufficient for generalization. Empirically, performance improvements on held-out datasets only emerge when models are exposed to multiple reformulations simultaneously, suggesting that diversity in difficulty is key. This observation motivates Cog-DRIFT, a framework that reformulates hard problems into a spectrum of variable difficulty problems, and organizes them into an adaptive curriculum analogous to cognitive scaffolding approaches in human education.2 2 2 We use the term “cognitive” to draw an analogy to human learning, referring specifically to structured progression in task difficulty rather than implying human-like cognition in models. Concretely, given a challenging open-ended question, we generate a family of cognitively simplified variants (e.g., multiple-choice with varying numbers of options, cloze-style questions), all of which preserve the original answer and thus maintain verifiable correctness. Rather than viewing these reformulations as isolated augmentations, we interpret them as forming a difficulty spectrum to enable ordered progression. This design also aligns with the Zone of Proximal Development in human learning, where learners benefit most from tasks that are appropriately difficult. Cog-DRIFT leverages this structure by adaptively advancing the model from easier to harder variants based on empirical accuracy (i.e., progressing to the next level once a target accuracy threshold is reached), thereby automating curriculum construction. We show that compared to static mixtures of reformulations, this adaptive curriculum yields more sample-efficient training.

We evaluate Cog-DRIFT on six benchmarks, including BigMath-Hard (Albalak et al., [2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib84 "Big-math: a large-scale, high-quality math dataset for reinforcement learning in language models")), OmniMATH-Hard (Gao et al., [2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib74 "Omni-math: a universal olympiad level mathematic benchmark for large language models")), AIME2024/2025 (AIME, [2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib73 "AIME. aime problems and solutions.")), GPQA-Diamond (Rein et al., [2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib64 "Gpqa: a graduate-level google-proof q&a benchmark")) and Date Understanding (Srivastava et al., [2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib100 "Beyond the imitation game: quantifying and extrapolating the capabilities of language models")) with two models: Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 (Team, [2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib56 "Qwen3 technical report")) and Llama3.2-3B-Instruct (Grattafiori et al., [2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib57 "The llama 3 herd of models")). Results demonstrate that reformulating hard problems to reduce cognitive load successfully unlocks learning where standard methods fail: we find that directly training on the hard problems using GRPO is not stable with sparse reward signals, and can harm generalization on held-out tasks. In contrast, Cog-DRIFT yields consistent gains across benchmarks: compared to the second-best baseline, our method shows an absolute +4.72% average improvement for Qwen, and +3.23% for Llama. Moreover, Cog-DRIFT also improves pass@k over both the base model and the GRPO-trained variant, suggesting that it enables the acquisition of new reasoning capabilities from hard examples that initially lie beyond the model’s capacity. We conduct extensive analyses and highlight two critical factors driving this success: (1) exposing the model to a diverse mixture of reformulations (such as combining multiple-choice and cloze formats) is essential for transferring reasoning skills back to open-ended tasks, and (2) an instance-level curriculum that progressively scales problem difficulty significantly boosts sample efficiency and prevents performance plateaus.

## 2 Related Work

Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR). RLVR has emerged as a dominant paradigm for training large language models on reasoning tasks by utilizing objective, rule-based feedback rather than human preferences (Lambert et al., [2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib1 "Tulu 3: pushing frontiers in open language model post-training"); Guo et al., [2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib2 "Deepseek-r1: incentivizing reasoning capability in llms via reinforcement learning"); Team et al., [2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib3 "Kimi k1. 5: scaling reinforcement learning with llms"); Zeng et al., [2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib34 "Simplerl-zoo: investigating and taming zero reinforcement learning for open base models in the wild")). By employing deterministic verifiers (e.g., binary rewards based on final answer correctness), RLVR is particularly effective for mathematics and code generation where ground truth is unambiguous. Extending this paradigm to unverifiable domains, Lu et al. ([2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib108 "Golden goose: a simple trick to synthesize unlimited rlvr tasks from unverifiable internet text")) demonstrate that large-scale internet text can be transformed into structured formats such as multiple-choice and masked prediction tasks to enable RLVR. Central to the current discourse is whether RLVR enables the genuine discovery of new reasoning abilities or primarily performs ”distribution sharpening” by amplifying high-reward paths already present in the model’s latent space (Zhang et al., [2025a](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib53 "A survey of reinforcement learning for large reasoning models"); Zhao et al., [2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib25 "Echo chamber: rl post-training amplifies behaviors learned in pretraining"); Shenfeld et al., [2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib51 "RL’s razor: why online reinforcement learning forgets less")). While some studies suggest RLVR mainly improves sampling efficiency (pass@1) without increasing the total knowledge (pass@k k) (Yue et al., [2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib24 "Does reinforcement learning really incentivize reasoning capacity in llms beyond the base model?"); He et al., [2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib27 "Rewarding the unlikely: lifting grpo beyond distribution sharpening")), others argue that RL can indeed foster new skills through the strategic combination of existing ones (Yuan et al., [2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib48 "From ⁢f(x) and ⁢g(x) to ⁢f(⁢g(x)): llms learn new skills in rl by composing old ones")) or by balancing the entropy-reward trade-off to prevent premature convergence (Wu et al., [2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib7 "The invisible leash: why rlvr may or may not escape its origin")). Although with a sufficiently large k k (e.g., vocab_size seq_len\text{vocab\_size}^{\text{seq\_len}}), even a randomly initialized model could achieve near-perfect pass@k k, RLVR operates under a rollout budget, so how to improve the model within a limited sampling budget, where k k must remain small relative to the total number of rollouts becomes the real question (Yu et al., [2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib105 "Unveiling implicit advantage symmetry: why grpo struggles with exploration and difficulty adaptation"); Nath et al., [2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib107 "Adaptive guidance accelerates reinforcement learning of reasoning models")).

Learning from the Hard Problems. A challenge in RLVR occurs when problems are excessively difficult such that on-policy sampling fails to discover any correct solutions, i.e., the model cannot get any non-zero reward (Qu et al., [2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib6 "Learning to reason on hard problems with privileged on-policy exploration")). To bridge this gap, research has shifted toward sophisticated exploration strategies and hybrid optimization. One line of work focuses on ”nudging” the model through self-generated hints, critiques, or partial solutions to reduce problem difficulty and expand the reasoning boundary (Li et al., [2025b](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib11 "Questa: expanding reasoning capacity in llms via question augmentation"); Zhang et al., [2025b](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib8 "Critique-grpo: advancing llm reasoning with natural language and numerical feedback"); Chen et al., [2025a](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib12 "Nudging the boundaries of llm reasoning"); Wang et al., [2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib83 "Beyond accuracy: dissecting mathematical reasoning for llms under reinforcement learning")). Ma et al. ([2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib106 "Learning what reinforcement learning can’t: interleaved online fine-tuning for hardest questions")) interleave SFT to learn from hard questions that RLVR cannot. Sundaram et al. ([2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib91 "Teaching models to teach themselves: reasoning at the edge of learnability")) train the teacher policy for generating question-answer pairs based on the improvement of the student policy. Other approaches leverage privileged information or offline trajectories to provide a learning signal in sparse-reward environments (Lu et al., [2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib77 "ARPO: end-to-end policy optimization for gui agents with experience replay"); Yan et al., [2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib42 "Learning to reason under off-policy guidance")), such as POPE, which uses offline prefixes from a stronger model to guide exploration into reachable reward regions (Qu et al., [2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib6 "Learning to reason on hard problems with privileged on-policy exploration")). In parallel, Sun et al. ([2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib13 "RL grokking recipe: how does rl unlock and transfer new algorithms in llms?")) propose a “grokking” recipe for RLVR by showing that staged warm-ups, experience replay, and a curriculum enable learnability on previously unsolvable problems. Together, these advancements suggest that scaling RLVR requires moving beyond pure on-policy exploration toward self-improvement mechanisms and careful curriculum design (Shi et al., [2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib111 "Efficient reinforcement finetuning via adaptive curriculum learning"); Parashar et al., [2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib109 "Curriculum reinforcement learning from easy to hard tasks improves llm reasoning"); Bae et al., [2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib110 "Online difficulty filtering for reasoning oriented reinforcement learning")). Despite their differences, these approaches share a common principle: they aim to make hard problems learnable by injecting additional signal – either through stronger supervision, guided exploration, or richer training dynamics. Our approach takes a fundamentally different perspective. We introduce cognitive load as a unifying lens and show that reformulating problems into simpler variants enables the model to access reward signals that are otherwise unreachable under standard RLVR. Crucially, this is coupled with an adaptive curriculum that dynamically schedules these reformulations based on the model’s learning progress, ensuring that training remains within a learnable regime while gradually increasing difficulty. Empirically, this leads to a substantial increase in the rate of hard problems during training that become solvable, reaching up to 18.9%, compared to the 1-4% range reported by prior methods (Chen et al., [2025a](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib12 "Nudging the boundaries of llm reasoning"); Sundaram et al., [2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib91 "Teaching models to teach themselves: reasoning at the edge of learnability")).

## 3 Problem Statement

Let ℳ θ\mathcal{M}_{\theta} denote a language model with parameters θ\theta. We consider a dataset of open-ended problems 𝒟={(x i,y i)}i=1 N\mathcal{D}=\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}, where x i x_{i} is a problem prompt and y i y_{i} is the corresponding exact answer.3 3 3 We use “open-ended” to denote problems with unconstrained output length, in contrast to multiple-choice or short-form tasks, while still assuming a well-defined correct answer. The model can generate k k independent samples {y^i(j)}j=1 k\{\hat{y}_{i}^{(j)}\}_{j=1}^{k} for a specific x i x_{i}, and the commonly used pass@k k metric evaluates whether at least one of the k k generations is correct:

pass@​k​(x i)=𝕀​[∃j∈{1,…,k}​such that​y^i(j)=y i].\text{pass@}k(x_{i})=\mathbb{I}[\exists j\in\{1,\dots,k\}\text{ such that }\hat{y}_{i}^{(j)}=y_{i}].

We define the set of _hard problems_ as the subset of examples on which the model fails to produce a correct answer even after 64 samples: 𝒟 hard={(x i,y i)∈𝒟∣pass@64​(x i)=0}.\mathcal{D}_{\text{hard}}=\{(x_{i},y_{i})\in\mathcal{D}\mid\text{pass@64}(x_{i})=0\}. This definition has an important implication for RL training with rollout-based objectives (e.g., GRPO): if pass@64​(x i)=0\text{pass@64}(x_{i})=0, then the probability of sampling a correct trajectory is extremely low (requiring ≫64\gg 64 samples) given the same policy. Consequently, the reward signal is generally non-existent: all sampled trajectories receive zero reward, leading to zero variance in the advantage estimates. This in turn prevents meaningful gradient updates, as no trajectory is preferred over others (Qu et al., [2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib6 "Learning to reason on hard problems with privileged on-policy exploration"); Yu et al., [2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib35 "Dapo: an open-source llm reinforcement learning system at scale")). Empirically, we show in [Section 6](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#S6 "6 Results and Analysis ‣ Cog-DRIFT: Exploration on Adaptively Reformulated Instances Enables Learning from Hard Reasoning Problems") that directly applying GRPO on 𝒟 hard\mathcal{D}_{\text{hard}} yields little to no improvement because the model rarely explores the correct trajectory in an unconstrained, open-ended output space. Moreover, training on 𝒟 hard\mathcal{D}_{\text{hard}} wastes computational effort performing rollouts on solutions with no hope of being correct. To address this, we introduce a task reformulation function 𝒯:(x,y)→(x′,y)\mathcal{T}:(x,y)\rightarrow(x^{\prime},y) that converts an open-ended problem into a cognitively simplified format while preserving the underlying solution. A successful reformulation should meet three criteria shown in [Section 3](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#S3 "3 Problem Statement ‣ Cog-DRIFT: Exploration on Adaptively Reformulated Instances Enables Learning from Hard Reasoning Problems"):

1.Answer preservation: must have the same correct answer as the original.2.Reduced difficulty: should make the problem empirically easier for the model.3.Transferability: Learning from the reformulated problem should improve performance on the original problem.

We explore several instantiations of 𝒯\mathcal{T}, utilizing an LLM (Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507) to generate the reformulated dataset 𝒟 hard′={(x i′,y i)∣(x i,y i)∈𝒟 hard}\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{\text{hard}}=\{(x^{\prime}_{i},y_{i})\mid(x_{i},y_{i})\in\mathcal{D}_{\text{hard}}\}. We provide the prompt for reformulation in [Appendix C](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#A3 "Appendix C Prompts for Reformulation ‣ Cog-DRIFT: Exploration on Adaptively Reformulated Instances Enables Learning from Hard Reasoning Problems"). The formats we consider include: (1) 4-Choice: The original question is combined with four answer options, where the gold answer appears in exactly one option. (2) 10-Choice: Same as above, but we expand the number of option choices to ten. (3) Cloze: The original question is combined with a partially masked answer. For example, if the answer is 101, we append “The answer should look like: 1__”. [Section 3](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#S3 "3 Problem Statement ‣ Cog-DRIFT: Exploration on Adaptively Reformulated Instances Enables Learning from Hard Reasoning Problems") is an example of an open-ended problem reformulated into a 4-choice format, and other examples for 10-choice and cloze can be found in [Appendix D](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#A4 "Appendix D Examples of Different Problem Formats ‣ Cog-DRIFT: Exploration on Adaptively Reformulated Instances Enables Learning from Hard Reasoning Problems"). Crucially, even after reformulation, the model must output the full answer value (e.g., 21) rather than just the option letter (e.g., B) for better transferability.

### 3.1 Evaluating Difficulty Reduction

By definition, all of our chosen reformulations satisfy criterion (1) by sharing the original gold answer in [Section 3](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#S3 "3 Problem Statement ‣ Cog-DRIFT: Exploration on Adaptively Reformulated Instances Enables Learning from Hard Reasoning Problems"). To evaluate criterion (2), we measure zero-shot performance on the reformulated dataset 𝒟 hard′\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{\text{hard}}, as shown in [Figure 2](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#S3.F2 "In 3 Problem Statement ‣ Cog-DRIFT: Exploration on Adaptively Reformulated Instances Enables Learning from Hard Reasoning Problems")(a). Because we exclusively sampled problems where pass@64 = 0, the baseline performance on the original open-ended questions is strictly 0.0. However, upon applying format changes, performance immediately surpasses the random guessing baseline. For instance, the 4-choice MCQ reformulation achieves 33.8% accuracy, well above the 25% random baseline. Similar trends hold for the 10-choice and cloze formats. We also observe that task reformulations induce a consistent ordering of empirical difficulty: MCQ 4≤MCQ 10≤Cloze≤Open-ended.\text{MCQ}_{4}\;\leq\;\text{MCQ}_{10}\;\leq\;\text{Cloze}\;\leq\;\text{Open-ended}.

### 3.2 Transferability and Generalization

Having established that these formats reduce difficulty, we evaluate criterion (3) in [Section 3](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#S3 "3 Problem Statement ‣ Cog-DRIFT: Exploration on Adaptively Reformulated Instances Enables Learning from Hard Reasoning Problems"): whether a model can learn from the converted problems and transfer those gains back to the original open-ended ones. To test this, we use 𝒟 hard′\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{\text{hard}} for rejection fine-tuning (RFT) (Yuan et al., [2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib70 "Scaling relationship on learning mathematical reasoning with large language models")), as depicted in [Figure 2](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#S3.F2 "In 3 Problem Statement ‣ Cog-DRIFT: Exploration on Adaptively Reformulated Instances Enables Learning from Hard Reasoning Problems")(b). Specifically, we use 𝒟 hard′\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{\text{hard}} to elicit correct chain-of-thought (Wei et al., [2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib30 "Chain of thought prompting elicits reasoning in large language models")) reasoning trajectories, perform supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on the correct {x′,y}\{x^{\prime},y\} pairs, and then evaluate the trained model on the original open-ended dataset 𝒟 hard\mathcal{D}_{\text{hard}}. While acquiring correct reasoning chains directly from 𝒟 hard\mathcal{D}_{\text{hard}} is not possible, the reduced difficulty of 𝒟 hard′\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{\text{hard}} allows us to successfully extract valid trajectories. The results show that models trained on 𝒟 hard′\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{\text{hard}} successfully transfer their learning back to 𝒟 hard\mathcal{D}_{\text{hard}}. Interestingly, in [Figure 2](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#S3.F2 "In 3 Problem Statement ‣ Cog-DRIFT: Exploration on Adaptively Reformulated Instances Enables Learning from Hard Reasoning Problems")(b), we also observe that the more the task is generative, the better the transfer back to the open-ended problem. For example, on the original 𝒟 hard\mathcal{D}_{\text{hard}}, model trained with 4-choice achieves 11.1%, 10 choice achieves 15.5%, and cloze achieves 18.9% accuracy. To isolate genuine reasoning capabilities from memorization, we evaluate the format-specific trained models on the unseen MATH500 benchmark (Lightman et al., [2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib63 "Let’s verify step by step"); Hendrycks et al., [2021](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib62 "Measuring mathematical problem solving with the math dataset")). As shown in [Figure 2](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#S3.F2 "In 3 Problem Statement ‣ Cog-DRIFT: Exploration on Adaptively Reformulated Instances Enables Learning from Hard Reasoning Problems")(c), models trained with different reformulations maintain fairly robust performance on this held-out dataset. Overall, the MCQ and cloze formats meet the criteria in [Section 3](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#S3 "3 Problem Statement ‣ Cog-DRIFT: Exploration on Adaptively Reformulated Instances Enables Learning from Hard Reasoning Problems"), and successfully (1) preserve the original answer, (2) reduce empirical difficulty, and (3) encourage the model to learn transferable, generalizable reasoning. Therefore, we select these formats for our main experiments.

## 4 Cog nitive D emand R eduction via I ncremental F ormat T ransitions

We build on the observation from [Section 3](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#S3 "3 Problem Statement ‣ Cog-DRIFT: Exploration on Adaptively Reformulated Instances Enables Learning from Hard Reasoning Problems") that different task reformulations form an order of empirical difficulty: MCQ 4≤MCQ 10≤Cloze≤Open-ended.\text{MCQ}_{4}\;\leq\;\text{MCQ}_{10}\;\leq\;\text{Cloze}\;\leq\;\text{Open-ended}. We leverage this structure to design a curriculum that adaptively increases problem difficulty based on the model’s competence. Specifically, given a hard problem (x i,y i)∈𝒟 hard(x_{i},y_{i})\in\mathcal{D}_{\text{hard}}, we construct a sequence of reformulated variants: {x i(1),x i(2),x i(3),x i(4)}\{x_{i}^{(1)},x_{i}^{(2)},x_{i}^{(3)},x_{i}^{(4)}\}, where each level corresponds to a format of increasing difficulty:

x i(1):4-choice MCQ,x i(2):10-choice MCQ,x i(3):Cloze,x i(4):Open-ended.x_{i}^{(1)}:\text{4-choice MCQ},\quad x_{i}^{(2)}:\text{10-choice MCQ},\quad x_{i}^{(3)}:\text{Cloze},\quad x_{i}^{(4)}:\text{Open-ended}.

All variants share the same ground-truth answer y i y_{i} and are generated via 𝒯\mathcal{T} introduced in [Section 3](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#S3 "3 Problem Statement ‣ Cog-DRIFT: Exploration on Adaptively Reformulated Instances Enables Learning from Hard Reasoning Problems"). We verify each reformulation deterministically via verification scripts to ensure correctness and validity. For MCQ, we verify that: (1) the gold answer appears among the options, and (2) it is uniquely correct (i.e., no other option corresponds to the gold answer). For cloze, we ensure that: (1) the masked span is non-trivial (i.e., not equivalent to the full gold answer), and (2) each revealed digit is consistent with the gold answer. For example, a gold answer of 101 and mask of 1_0 would constitute a failure case.

Instance-level Curriculum. We associate each training example with a difficulty level d i∈{1,2,3,4}d_{i}\in\{1,2,3,4\}, initialized to the easiest level d i=1 d_{i}=1. During training, the model ℳ θ\mathcal{M}_{\theta} generates m m samples for the current format: {y^i(j)}j=1 m∼ℳ θ(⋅∣x i(d i))\{\hat{y}_{i}^{(j)}\}_{j=1}^{m}\sim\mathcal{M}_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x_{i}^{(d_{i})}). We estimate the empirical accuracy at level d i d_{i} as: a^i=1 m​∑j=1 m 𝕀​[y^i(j)=y i]\hat{a}_{i}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathbb{I}[\hat{y}_{i}^{(j)}=y_{i}]. If a^i≥τ\hat{a}_{i}\geq\tau, we promote the example to the next difficulty level. We adopt τ=0.5\tau=0.5 in our experiments following past work (Chen et al., [2025b](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib112 "Self-evolving curriculum for llm reasoning"); Foster et al., [2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib113 "Learning to reason at the frontier of learnability")). Otherwise, the example remains at the current level. This creates a per-sample curriculum that adapts to the model’s evolving capability, ensuring that training focuses on the most informative difficulty for each problem.

GRPO Training Objective. Given recent advancements driven by RLVR, we optimize the model using Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) with rule-based rewards (Guo et al., [2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib2 "Deepseek-r1: incentivizing reasoning capability in llms via reinforcement learning"); Shao et al., [2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib17 "Deepseekmath: pushing the limits of mathematical reasoning in open language models")). For each input x i(d i)x_{i}^{(d_{i})}, we sample a group of m m responses and compute rewards for each sample. Let r i(j)r_{i}^{(j)} denote the reward for y^i(j)\hat{y}_{i}^{(j)}. GRPO normalizes rewards within the group, and the objective is:

ℒ GRPO​(θ)=−𝔼 i,j​[r~i(j)​log⁡ℳ θ​(y^i(j)∣x i(d i))]\mathcal{L}_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta)=-\mathbb{E}_{i,j}\left[\tilde{r}_{i}^{(j)}\log\mathcal{M}_{\theta}(\hat{y}_{i}^{(j)}\mid x_{i}^{(d_{i})})\right]

which encourages the model to assign higher probability to relatively better samples within each group. Our reward function combines correctness and format compliance: r i(j)=r correct​(y^i(j),y i)+r format​(y^i(j))r_{i}^{(j)}=r_{\text{correct}}(\hat{y}_{i}^{(j)},y_{i})+r_{\text{format}}(\hat{y}_{i}^{(j)}), where r correct∈[0,1]r_{\text{correct}}\in[0,1] indicates whether the predicted answer matches the ground truth, r format∈[0,0.2]r_{\text{format}}\in[0,0.2] measures whether the output follows the required format, i.e., whether the final answer is found in \boxed{}(Guo et al., [2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib2 "Deepseek-r1: incentivizing reasoning capability in llms via reinforcement learning")).

## 5 Experimental Setup

Models. We evaluate Cog-DRIFT on two models: Llama3.2-3B-Instruct (Grattafiori et al., [2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib57 "The llama 3 herd of models")) and Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 (Team, [2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib56 "Qwen3 technical report")).4 4 4 Hereafter, we refer to them as Llama and Qwen, respectively. We consider these two models because they show different behaviors in post-training (Shao et al., [2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib49 "Spurious rewards: rethinking training signals in rlvr")).

Dataset. For training, we use the BigMath dataset (Albalak et al., [2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib84 "Big-math: a large-scale, high-quality math dataset for reinforcement learning in language models")) due to its diversity and inclusion of difficulty annotation. Specifically, it contains 251k samples along with the pass rate using Llama3-8B, making it easier to start with a harder subset with a lower pass rate, and sample 64 reasoning chains to obtain our 𝒟 hard\mathcal{D}_{\text{hard}}. We start with the hardest 20% samples (37.5k), and let Qwen sample 64 reasoning chains, only keeping the samples for which _none of the 64 chains have reached the correct solution_. Since Qwen is generally outperforming Llama, we use Qwen to obtain 𝒟 hard\mathcal{D}_{\text{hard}} and use the same 𝒟 hard\mathcal{D}_{\text{hard}} for Llama. This results in 8.9k samples. We notice that using pass@64 alone causes selection bias to include problems that are actually unsolvable, such as incomplete questions or referring to a figure that does not exist (examples shown in [Appendix E](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#A5 "Appendix E Example of Truly Unsolvable Problems ‣ Cog-DRIFT: Exploration on Adaptively Reformulated Instances Enables Learning from Hard Reasoning Problems")). To alleviate this, we keep problems for which GPT5.4’s majority answer over three runs agrees with the gold answer (OpenAI, [2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib61 "Introducing gpt‑5.4")). This leads to our final filtered training data, consisting of 958 hard problems (more details in [Section 6](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#S6 "6 Results and Analysis ‣ Cog-DRIFT: Exploration on Adaptively Reformulated Instances Enables Learning from Hard Reasoning Problems")). For evaluation, we test Cog-DRIFT on six benchmarks, including (1) BigMathHard (BMH), (2) OmniMATH-Hard, (3) AIME 24/25, (4) GPQA Diamond, (5) Date Understanding. Details on each evaluation dataset are provided in [Appendix B](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#A2 "Appendix B Dataset Statistics and Licenses ‣ Cog-DRIFT: Exploration on Adaptively Reformulated Instances Enables Learning from Hard Reasoning Problems").

Baselines. We mainly compare with baselines that do not need privileged information or require a stronger teacher model: (1) Zero-shot: We prompt the model to think step-by-step and provide the answer within \boxed{} (Kojima et al., [2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib22 "Large language models are zero-shot reasoners")). (2) Few-shot: Besides prompting the model to think step-by-step, we include 8 in-context learning samples. (3) Rejection sampling Fine-Tuning (RFT): We prompt the model with cloze reformulated problems, and use the correct trajectories to perform supervised fine-tuning (Yuan et al., [2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib70 "Scaling relationship on learning mathematical reasoning with large language models")). We choose fill-in-the-blank here because it performs the best (c.f. [Figure 2](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#S3.F2 "In 3 Problem Statement ‣ Cog-DRIFT: Exploration on Adaptively Reformulated Instances Enables Learning from Hard Reasoning Problems")). (4) GRPO: Train on 𝒟 hard\mathcal{D_{\text{hard}}} using outcome correctness as the reward (Guo et al., [2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib2 "Deepseek-r1: incentivizing reasoning capability in llms via reinforcement learning")). (5) NuRL (Abstract): we use abstract cues that are generated from answer explanations using 𝒟 hard\mathcal{D_{\text{hard}}} to hint the model during GRPO (Chen et al., [2025a](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib12 "Nudging the boundaries of llm reasoning")). (6) NuRL (Prefix): we use partial steps as hints instead, similar to POPE (Qu et al., [2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib6 "Learning to reason on hard problems with privileged on-policy exploration")) except the steps are also self-generated.

Cog-DRIFT achieves the strongest overall results, improving avg. accuracy by +4.72% (Qwen) and +3.23% (Llama). Crucially, it unlocks gains on BMH (0 → 10.11% on Qwen / 8.64% on Llama), where all the problems are collected with pass@64=0.

Implementation Details. We evaluate all methods using pass@1. Results are averaged over 16 runs for AIME, and 3 runs for other datasets. We adopt verl (Sheng et al., [2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib69 "HybridFlow: a flexible and efficient rlhf framework")) as the backbone, and utilize vllm (Kwon et al., [2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib68 "Efficient memory management for large language model serving with pagedattention")) to speed up rollout generation and inference. To verify equivalence between predictions and references, we use Math-Verify.5 5 5[https://github.com/huggingface/Math-Verify](https://github.com/huggingface/Math-Verify) Other hyperparameters and details can be found in [Appendix A](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#A1 "Appendix A Implementation Details ‣ Cog-DRIFT: Exploration on Adaptively Reformulated Instances Enables Learning from Hard Reasoning Problems") and in our code.

## 6 Results and Analysis

Reformulation enables learning from the hard problems. In [Table 1](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#S5.T1 "In 5 Experimental Setup ‣ Cog-DRIFT: Exploration on Adaptively Reformulated Instances Enables Learning from Hard Reasoning Problems"), zero-shot baselines achieve 0% performance on BigMath-Hard (BMH), as expected for a pass@64=0 dataset. As discussed in [Section 3](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#S3 "3 Problem Statement ‣ Cog-DRIFT: Exploration on Adaptively Reformulated Instances Enables Learning from Hard Reasoning Problems"), while RFT improves BMH performance, it generalizes poorly due to distribution shift from cloze-style training. In principle, GRPO should not yield learning gains when trained on samples with pass@64 = 0. However, with Qwen, we observe a +6.37% improvement on BMH. This may be attributed to the spurious reward effect specific to Qwen (Shao et al., [2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib49 "Spurious rewards: rethinking training signals in rlvr")), where learning can occur even under weak or uninformative reward signals. Another hypothesis is that occasional successful rollouts (arising from stochastic exploration) introduce sparse but amplified reward signals, causing the model to overfit to a narrow subset of problems without learning transferable reasoning strategies. In contrast, Llama shows no improvement under GRPO, with BMH performance remaining at 0% after training. Despite gains on Qwen, GRPO harms generalization, causing drops across held-out benchmarks. NuRL with abstract hints (Abstract) or partial solutions (Prefix) provides a middle ground: it yields modest BMH gains and occasional improvements on held-out datasets, likely due to preserving format and leveraging in-domain, self-generated hints. By contrast, Cog-DRIFT outperforms all baselines, improving BMH by an absolute +10.11% (Qwen) and +8.64% (Llama), while also boosting five held-out benchmarks with average gains of 4.72% and 3.23%, respectively. This demonstrates that reformulation enables effective learning with strong cross-task generalization.

Cog-DRIFT improves pass@k. In [Table 1](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#S5.T1 "In 5 Experimental Setup ‣ Cog-DRIFT: Exploration on Adaptively Reformulated Instances Enables Learning from Hard Reasoning Problems"), we observe that Cog-DRIFT consistently outperforms baseline methods in terms of pass@1. Because our approach specifically targets problems with pass@64 = 0 – i.e., problems that are unsolvable under standard training and therefore contain knowledge inaccessible to the model – we hypothesize that successful learning from these examples should also manifest as improvements in pass@k k for larger values of k k, reflecting the acquisition of new knowledge. To test this hypothesis, we plot pass@k k for k∈1,2,…,128 k\in{1,2,\ldots,128} in [Figure 3](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#S6.F3 "In 6 Results and Analysis ‣ Cog-DRIFT: Exploration on Adaptively Reformulated Instances Enables Learning from Hard Reasoning Problems") using Qwen. On AIME2024, AIME2025, and GPQA, Cog-DRIFT consistently achieves higher pass@k k as k k increases, outperforming both the base instruct model and the GRPO-trained model. At k=128 k=128, Cog-DRIFT improves over the base model by +3.33%, +3.33%, and +2.02% on AIME2024, AIME2025, and GPQA, respectively. In contrast, GRPO again exhibits degraded performance, echoing the finding that directly training on hard problems fails to generalize. These results indicate that learning from reformulated problems reshapes the model’s output distribution, making correct solutions more accessible under sampling. Overall, the gains in pass@k k provide further evidence that training on reformulated hard problems can effectively learn reasoning capabilities.

Using multiple reformulations is crucial. To better understand the transfer effect from reformulated variants to the original open-ended problems, we analyze how different data mixtures influence performance. Specifically, we remove the curriculum setup and instead rejection fine-tune (RFT) the models on various combinations of problem types. [Table 2](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#S6.T2 "In 6 Results and Analysis ‣ Cog-DRIFT: Exploration on Adaptively Reformulated Instances Enables Learning from Hard Reasoning Problems") reports the performance on OmniMATH-Hard and MATH500 under different mixtures. The first three rows show that training with a single reformulation does not improve over no training. Notably, training solely on 4 Choice + Cloze without any open-ended data achieves improved performance, and it is comparable to mixtures that include open-ended problems. Therefore, we conclude that diversity in problem representation is critical for effective transfer and for learning from hard problems, and that including more discriminative formats (e.g., 4 Choice + Cloze) is crucial.

Instance-Level curriculum improves sample efficiency. In [Table 2](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#S6.T2 "In 6 Results and Analysis ‣ Cog-DRIFT: Exploration on Adaptively Reformulated Instances Enables Learning from Hard Reasoning Problems"), we show that training on a mixture of multiple reformulations is already effective for learning from hard problems. However, each reformulation increases the dataset size linearly; for example, combining OEQ, 4-choice MCQ, and Cloze triples the number of training samples. Since these reformulations largely share the same underlying information (differing primarily in structure and cognitive load), we hypothesize that a more adaptive curriculum can improve sample efficiency. In [Figure 4](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#S6.F4 "In 6 Results and Analysis ‣ Cog-DRIFT: Exploration on Adaptively Reformulated Instances Enables Learning from Hard Reasoning Problems"), we present both the proportion of each problem type during training and the corresponding test performance on OmniMATH-Hard, comparing settings with and without our instance-level curriculum. We refer to this approach as instance-level because progression is determined per instance: once an instance achieves an average accuracy above 50%, it is automatically advanced to the next difficulty level. As a result, the proportion of 4-choice MCQ (the easiest format) steadily decreases over training, while intermediate formats (10-choice MCQ and Cloze) first increase and then decline, and open-ended questions grow steadily as the final stage of the curriculum. In contrast, without the curriculum, the proportion of each problem type remains uniform throughout training (25% each). Importantly, the curriculum leads to continued improvements in test performance on OmniMATH-Hard, whereas performance without the curriculum tends to plateau.

Estimating problem difficulty with pass@k can introduce selection bias. While using pass@k is a common way to estimate problem difficulty (Yuan et al., [2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib48 "From ⁢f(x) and ⁢g(x) to ⁢f(⁢g(x)): llms learn new skills in rl by composing old ones"); Jiang et al., [2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib102 "FATE: a formal benchmark series for frontier algebra of multiple difficulty levels"); Yu et al., [2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib35 "Dapo: an open-source llm reinforcement learning system at scale")), it also comes naturally with selection bias, which includes problems that are “truly unsolvable” not just because the model is not capable of. In particular, we observe that our initial dataset 𝒟 hard\mathcal{D}_{\text{hard}} contains a substantial number of flawed samples, including incomplete or unanswerable questions, as well as instances with incorrect ground-truth labels. To quantify the impact of data quality, we evaluate two filtering strategies: (1) Binary Filter, where GPT-5.4 (OpenAI, [2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib61 "Introducing gpt‑5.4")) is prompted to determine whether a question is complete and answerable, and only samples labeled as solvable are retained – without requiring the model to produce a solution; and (2) Silver Answer Filter, where GPT-5.4 generates three independent solutions and we retain only those samples whose ground-truth answer agrees with the majority vote. The resulting dataset statistics and corresponding performance are summarized in [Table 3](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#S6.T3 "In 6 Results and Analysis ‣ Cog-DRIFT: Exploration on Adaptively Reformulated Instances Enables Learning from Hard Reasoning Problems"). The Silver Answer Filter is highly selective, removing a large fraction of the data, whereas the Binary Filter yields a more moderate reduction. Despite these differences, Qwen shows to be less sensitive to data quality: its performance on MATH500 remains largely unchanged across filtering strategies, with only minor variation on GPQA. We suspect that this might be relevant to its unique characteristics that can even improve with random reward (Shao et al., [2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib49 "Spurious rewards: rethinking training signals in rlvr")). In contrast, Llama is more sensitive to dataset quality, showing a wider performance range across settings and a consistent trend of improved results with cleaner data. Therefore, we apply the Silver Answer Filter by default. Notably, GPT-5.4 is used solely to approximate the filtering of noisy question–answer pairs; we do not use its reasoning traces for any form of distillation.

Table 3: Impact of data quality filtering on model performance. While the Silver Answer Filter is more selective and substantially reduces dataset size, it consistently improves performance for Llama and slightly for Qwen on GPQA. Overall, Llama is more sensitive to data quality, whereas Qwen is less affected by variations in data quality.

## 7 Conclusion

In this work, we investigate how to enable language models to learn effectively from problems that lie beyond their current capabilities. Drawing inspiration from theories of human learning, we leverage cognitive load as a principled mechanism for adjusting problem difficulty, thereby bringing challenging tasks within a model’s Zone of Proximal Development. To this end, we propose Cog-DRIFT, a framework that reformulates hard, open-ended questions into cognitively simplified variants that preserve the same correct answer and knowledge tested, while making them more tractable. Across two models and six challenging benchmarks, we show that Cog-DRIFT not only improves pass@1 performance over strong baselines, but also generalizes effectively, and with gains extending to higher values of k k. This suggests that the model is genuinely learning from problems that naive approaches fail to exploit. Furthermore, we demonstrate that curriculum design plays a critical role in sample efficiency: our instance-level curriculum continues to yield improvements over time, whereas static data mixtures quickly plateau. Overall, our results highlight problem reformulation as a simple yet powerful strategy for reducing task difficulty, offering a practical pathway to unlock learning from previously intractable problems.

## Appendix A Implementation Details

All methods are run on 4 A6000 GPUs. During training, we use a temperature of 1.0 and set the clip-high parameter ϵ\epsilon to 0.28 (Yu et al., [2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib35 "Dapo: an open-source llm reinforcement learning system at scale")). The rollout number is 8 for every GRPO-based method. We cap the output length at 8192 tokens for both training and testing. At inference, we fix the temperature to 0.7. Token limits and inference temperature are aligned across all baselines for evaluation. We report the detailed hyperparameters in [Table 4](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#A1.T4 "In Appendix A Implementation Details ‣ Cog-DRIFT: Exploration on Adaptively Reformulated Instances Enables Learning from Hard Reasoning Problems").

## Appendix B Dataset Statistics and Licenses

Cog-DRIFT is tested across a diverse set of reasoning-intensive tasks spanning STEM and other domain-specific areas, including: (1) BigMathHard (BMH), a pass@64=0 test split with 192 samples from BigMath (Albalak et al., [2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib84 "Big-math: a large-scale, high-quality math dataset for reinforcement learning in language models")). (2) OmniMATH-Hard, the hardest (difficulty ≥\geq 7.5) 452 Olympiad-level math problems from OmniMATH (Gao et al., [2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib74 "Omni-math: a universal olympiad level mathematic benchmark for large language models")); (3) AIME 24/25, 30 mathematics problems from the 2024/2025 AIME competition (AIME, [2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib73 "AIME. aime problems and solutions.")); (4) GPQA Diamond, 198 PhD-level questions covering biology, physics, and chemistry (Rein et al., [2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib64 "Gpqa: a graduate-level google-proof q&a benchmark")); (5) Date Understanding, 369 date and time arithmetic tasks from Big-Bench (Srivastava et al., [2022](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib100 "Beyond the imitation game: quantifying and extrapolating the capabilities of language models")). Some of the analyses are also run on MATH500, a subset 500 problems from the MATH benchmark (Hendrycks et al., [2021](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib62 "Measuring mathematical problem solving with the math dataset")) curated by Lightman et al. ([2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib63 "Let’s verify step by step")); Note that GPQA and Date Understanding consist of multiple choice questions, but we evaluate the model with answer match (Chandak et al., [2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#bib.bib96 "Answer matching outperforms multiple choice for language model evaluation")), i.e., the generated answer should match the ground truth answer, instead of just comparing the option choice. This mitigates the tendency to generate a shortcut answer or memorize the option choice. For Date Understanding which questions are easier, we remove option choices to make it fully open-ended. We provide the sample sizes and licenses of the datasets used in this work in [Table 5](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04767#A2.T5 "In Appendix B Dataset Statistics and Licenses ‣ Cog-DRIFT: Exploration on Adaptively Reformulated Instances Enables Learning from Hard Reasoning Problems").
