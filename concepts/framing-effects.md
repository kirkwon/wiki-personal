# Framing Effects

Framing effects describe how the presentation of choices systematically alters decisions, even when the underlying options are identical. The same options presented differently—such as in gain vs. loss frames, with different default options, or with varying choice‑set compositions—lead people to make different choices. Two options that are logically equivalent may elicit very different responses depending on whether they are described in terms of gains, losses, survival rates, or mortality rates.

Annie Duke emphasizes in *How to Decide* that the way a decision is framed greatly influences perceptions and outcomes, and that strategies for reframing can improve results. Daniel Kahneman demonstrates through experiments that people respond differently to a surgery described as having a “90 % survival rate” versus a “10 % mortality rate,” even though these statements are mathematically identical. This phenomenon reveals that [[system-1-vs-system-2-thinking]] processes the emotional tone of a description before engaging in logical evaluation.

## Key Mechanisms

- **Gain vs. Loss Frame** – People are risk‑averse when options are framed as gains and risk‑seeking when framed as losses  
- **Default Options** – Choices presented as defaults are selected far more often than opt‑in alternatives  
- **Choice Set Composition** – The presence or absence of certain options alters preferences for the remaining options  
- **Reference Points** – Decisions are evaluated relative to a reference point, which can be shifted by presentation  
- **Emotional Processing** – The emotional tone of a description is processed before logical evaluation, as shown by [[system-1-vs-system-2-thinking]]

## Theoretical Foundations

Framing effects are central to [[prospect-theory]], which shows that people evaluate outcomes relative to a reference point and are more sensitive to losses than gains. The way a problem is framed shifts that reference point and changes the perceived landscape of risk and reward. This reveals that preferences are constructed, not stable.

## Reframing Strategies & Interaction with Other Biases

- **Reframing Decisions** – Strategies exist for deliberately reframing a decision to lead to better outcomes; awareness of how a choice is presented is a prerequisite for more objective decision‑making.  
- **Interaction with Other Biases** – Framing interacts with other cognitive biases, such as [[confirmation-bias]] and [[ascription-bias]], amplifying or mitigating their effects.

## Implications

Framing effects have major implications across domains:
- **Public policy design** – Nudging through defaults  
- **Marketing and product presentation**  
- **Negotiation strategy**  
- **Personal decision‑making**  
- **Medical decisions** – Heavily influenced by how prognosis is communicated  
- **Financial choices** – Change when returns are framed as gains vs. avoided losses  
- **Policy preferences** – Shift when the same information is presented as lives saved vs. lives lost  

Framing effects are one of the most robust and well‑documented cognitive biases, with implications across medicine, economics, law, and everyday decision‑making.

Thaler’s work on framing connects to [[prospect-theory]] (Kahneman & Tversky), [[choice-overload]], and [[ethical-design-manipulation-question]]—the tension between using framing to help people versus manipulating them. This concept is closely related to the broader [[cognitive-biases-library]].
