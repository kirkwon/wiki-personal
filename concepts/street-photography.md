# Street Photography

Street photography is candid photography of public life, capturing unplanned, unposed moments in public spaces to tell stories about people, culture, and urban life. The practice emphasizes presence, anticipation, observation, quick shooting, and candid framing, along with the art of anticipating decisive moments over technical perfection.

## Key Characteristics

- **Candid**: Subjects are unaware or unposed
- **Public**: Takes place in streets, parks, markets, and other public areas
- **Storytelling**: Images convey narrative or emotion
- **Documentary**: Captures a slice of life, era, and culture

## Philosophy

Street photography is defined by capturing unposed, unplanned moments of public life. The most critical factor in street photography is being present and ready — the principle captured by the famous quote "F8 and be there," attributed to Weegee. Technical skills matter less than anticipation, positioning, and the willingness to engage with the scene. The goal is to capture what Henri Cartier-Bresson called the "decisive moment" — the perfect instant when all visual elements come together. Capturing it requires anticipating behavior, positioning oneself for the best vantage point, waiting patiently, and shooting quickly when the moment unfolds.

## Ethics

Respect is foundational to street photography. Practitioners should:
- Know legal rights in public spaces (varies by country)
- Avoid photographing vulnerable people (children in distress, homeless individuals)
- Respect cultural norms and local laws
- Cease photographing if someone objects
- Never photograph in private spaces without permission
- Consider commercial use may require model releases

## Equipment

### Camera Choice

| Camera Type | Pros | Cons | Best for |
|-------------|------|------|----------|
| **Mirrorless/DSLR** | Fast autofocus, good lenses | Large, conspicuous | Serious street photographers |
| **Compact camera** | Portable, discreet | Smaller sensor | Most street photographers |
| **Film rangefinder** | Quiet, classic | No preview, film costs | Traditional approach |
| **Phone** | Always available, discreet | Limited control | Casual street photography |

### Recommended Lenses

| Focal Length | Field of View | Why Good for Street |
|--------------|----------------|-------------------|
| **35mm** | Wide normal | Context + intimacy, classic street length |
| **28mm** | Wide | Environmental portraits, lots of context |
| **50mm** | Normal | Natural perspective, good compression |
| **24mm** | Very wide | Environmental, lots of context |

The street photography sweet spot is 35mm for balancing context and intimacy.

The ideal street photography setup prioritizes discretion and speed:
- **Focal length**: 35mm is the classic sweet spot (context + intimacy)
- **Aperture**: f/8 – f/11 for wide depth of field
- **Shutter**: 1/250 – 1/500 to freeze motion
- **Camera**: Compact or mirrorless preferred for discretion
- **Accessories**: Comfortable strap, lens hood, extra battery, fast memory card

### Settings

Recommended street photography settings prioritize speed and discretion:
- **Mode**: Manual or Aperture Priority
- **Aperture**: f/8 – f/11 (wide depth of field for candid shots)
- **Shutter**: 1/250 – 1/500 (freeze motion)
- **ISO**: Auto (up to 3200)
- **Focus**: Zone focus or AF-C (continuous) for fast, pre-focused shooting
- **Drive mode**: Continuous
- **Metering**: Evaluative

See [[photography-fundamentals]] for more detailed genre-specific settings.

## Techniques

### Zone Focusing

Zone focusing involves pre-focusing to a set distance (typically 6–10 feet) with a wide aperture (f/8 – f/16), then shooting without refocusing. This technique is faster than autofocus, eliminates delay between seeing and shooting, and is discreet since there is no beep or focus light. Pre-focusing to a set distance means subjects moving into that zone are in focus without waiting for autofocus, making it especially useful for fast candid shooting.

### Hyperfocal Distance

Hyperfocal distance is the closest distance at which a lens can focus while keeping infinity sharp. Setting focus at this distance ensures everything from half that distance to infinity remains in focus, providing maximum depth of field for pre-focused shooting.

### The Decisive Moment

Coined by Henri Cartier-Bresson, the decisive moment is the perfect instant when all visual elements come together. Capturing it requires anticipating behavior, positioning oneself for the best vantage point, waiting patiently, and shooting quickly when the moment unfolds. See [[decisive-moment]] for more.

### Layered Scenes

Layered scenes create depth and interest by composing with three elements: a foreground element (person close to camera), middle ground (main subject), and background (context and environment). See [[layered-scenes]] for more.

### Street Portraits

Two approaches exist: candid portraits (photographing people without their knowledge, requiring quick shutter and discreet camera) and asked portraits (approaching people respectfully, being specific and brief, offering to share the photo).

### F8 and Be There

The philosophy attributed to Weegee emphasizes that the most important factor in street photography is being present and ready to capture moments. Technical skills matter less than presence. See [[f8-and-be-there]].

### Golden Hour for Street Photography

Golden hour is an ideal time for street photography because:
- Warm light on buildings and people
- Long shadows create dimension
- Golden light on windows
- People going about their day in beautiful light

See [[golden-hour]] for timing and technique guidance.

## Composition

Street photography composition leverages urban elements:
- **Leading lines**: Roads, sidewalks, building edges, shadows, reflections
- **Framing**: Doorways, windows, alleys, overpasses, tunnels, signs
- **Reflections**: Store windows, car windows, puddles, mirrors, polished metal
- **Patterns and repetition**: Rows of windows, repeating shapes, geometric architecture
- **Layered scenes**: Foreground, middle ground, and background creating depth

## Locations

Best locations include busy streets (rush hour), markets, transportation hubs, parks, tourist areas, neighborhoods, and events. See [[san-francisco-city-guide]] for San Francisco-specific locations including Mission District (murals, Latino culture), Chinatown (dense, colorful activity), North Beach (Italian restaurants, nightlife), Fisherman's Wharf (tourists, waterfront), Castro (gay culture, nightlife), Haight-Ashbury (vintage shops, counterculture), SoMa (urban grit, tech culture), and Sunset District (fog, beaches). [[Sunset District]] and [[Ocean Beach]] are particularly noted for golden hour opportunities.

## Processing

Street photography favors minimal processing. Basic adjustments include exposure, contrast, and cropping. Black and white conversion is often preferred because it removes color distraction, emphasizes light and shadow, provides a timeless look, and focuses attention on emotion and moment. See [[post-processing]] for more on editing.

## Practice

Daily practice involves walking the same route, setting themes (shadows, reflections, people with hats), limiting equipment or location, and reviewing work after a time limit. Exercises include walking the same route to observe changes, setting thematic constraints (shadows, reflections), limiting to one lens or location, and reviewing work ruthlessly. Weekly practice includes project-based themes, studying master photographers, and visiting new locations. Monthly practice involves creating photo series, photographing events, and seeking critique. Studying master photographers like Henri Cartier-Bresson, Robert Frank, Garry Winogrand, Alex Webb, and Bruce Gilden is also encouraged.
