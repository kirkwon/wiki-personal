# Heuristic Problem-Solving

Heuristic problem-solving refers to the use of general-purpose strategies—rather than fixed algorithms—to tackle unfamiliar problems. George Polya's *How to Solve It* provides what he calls a "dictionary of heuristic," a collection of dozens of such strategies for each phase of problem-solving.

Key heuristics include:

- **Analogy** — Using similar solved problems as templates for new ones.
- **Decomposing and Recombining** — Breaking complex problems into simpler parts, solving each, then reassembling.
- **Working Backwards / Taking the Problem As Solved** — Starting from the goal and reasoning backward to the given information.
- **Specialization and Generalization** — Examining specific cases to extract general principles, then extending them.
- **Auxiliary Elements** — Introducing new variables, diagrams, or constructs to make the problem tractable.
- **Inventor's Paradox** — The counterintuitive insight that more ambitious problems can sometimes be easier than restricted ones.
- **Bright Idea / Insight** — The moment of solution recognition, which Polya argues emerges from systematic engagement rather than randomness.
- **Reductio ad Absurdum** — Proof by contradiction, a classical logical technique.
- **Variation of the Problem** — Modifying the problem to gain insight.

These heuristics connect to [[recognition-primed-decision-model]], where Klein emphasizes pattern recognition from experience. Polya similarly asks "Have you seen this problem before?"—a shared emphasis on leveraging prior knowledge. They also relate to [[decision-making-frameworks]], providing a structured alternative to probabilistic approaches like [[thinking-in-bets---annie-duke]].
