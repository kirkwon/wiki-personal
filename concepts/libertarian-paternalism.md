# Libertarian Paternalism

Libertarian paternalism, often called "nudging," is the ethical framework and philosophical position underlying nudge theory. It holds that it is possible to guide people toward better decisions while preserving their freedom to choose otherwise. Rather than restricting options or mandating behavior, it alters the choice architecture — the way options are presented, ordered, and defaulted — to make better outcomes more likely.

## Core Argument

Thaler and Sunstein argue that since choice environments are always present—someone always sets defaults, orders options, frames choices—there is no neutral design. Therefore, it is better to design environments that account for human biases than to ignore them. These interventions can significantly improve outcomes in retirement savings, organ donation, health decisions, and financial products without removing freedom of choice.

## Key Principles

- Freedom of choice is preserved — individuals can always opt out
- The environment in which choices are made can be deliberately designed
- Defaults and structural arrangements can guide behavior without coercion
- The "libertarian" element ensures no options are eliminated
- The "paternalism" element means the choice environment is designed to promote welfare
- Choice architectures can be optimized through simplifying information presentation and setting optimal default options (e.g., automatic enrollment in retirement savings plans)

## Mechanisms

Key mechanisms include:
- Setting optimal default options (e.g., automatic enrollment in retirement savings plans)
- Simplifying information presentation
- Framing choices in ways that account for known cognitive biases

## Distinction from Other Approaches

Unlike traditional paternalism, libertarian paternalism does not mandate behavior. Unlike pure libertarianism, it acknowledges that choice environments have real effects on outcomes.

## Ethical Debates

Critics raise concerns about manipulation, the assumption that designers know what is best, and the potential for nudges to serve institutional interests rather than individual welfare. The ethical tension lies between improving outcomes and respecting autonomy — nudging works with human psychology rather than against it, but it raises questions about who decides what counts as "better."
