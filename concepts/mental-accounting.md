# Mental Accounting

Mental accounting is a concept introduced by Richard Thaler describing how people organize, evaluate, and keep track of financial activities in ways that differ from traditional economic theory. The key insight is that money is not fungible in people's minds—they treat different categories of money differently, leading to systematically suboptimal financial decisions. People do not evaluate financial decisions based on total wealth — instead they create separate "accounts" for earned income, windfalls, savings, and spending, and make irrational decisions based on these arbitrary categories. This tendency for people to compartmentalize their finances into separate mental accounts rather than making holistic decisions about their overall financial wellbeing explains why individuals often make suboptimal financial decisions despite having rational tools available.

## Core Findings

- People treat "found money" (windfalls, refunds, tax returns) differently from earned money
- Spending is categorized into arbitrary mental accounts (vacation fund, entertainment, travel fund, etc.)
- People evaluate outcomes relative to mental account boundaries rather than total wealth
- Spending decisions are influenced by which "account" the money comes from, rather than by overall wealth or need
- Budgeting for individual categories rather than optimizing across total resources
- People feel pain from spending in one account while being indifferent in another
- This violates the economic principle of fungibility—money should be interchangeable regardless of source or category
- This compartmentalization leads to ignoring fungibility of money—treating equivalent sums differently based on arbitrary mental categories

## Examples

- Spending a tax refund differently than an equivalent amount of earned income
- Treating a gambling loss as "house money" and continuing to gamble
- Keeping a separate savings account mentally even though it earns the same return as other investments
- Rejecting an asymmetric deal (e.g., losing $100 on one bet, winning $150 on another) while accepting two separate symmetric deals
- Spending a windfall (bonus, refund) more freely than equivalent earnings
- Keeping a "travel fund" separate from general savings, even when general savings would be more efficient
- Treating investment gains as "house money" and taking on more risk with them
- Spending a bonus freely while refusing to spend from savings
- Holding losing investments too long because they are tracked in a "winning" mental account

## Connection to Decision Quality

Mental accounting undermines [[decision-quality-vs-outcome-quality]] by encouraging decisions that feel rational within an account but are suboptimal in aggregate. It relates to [[endowment-effect]] in that people overvalue money already assigned to a specific account. The importance of evaluating decisions on process rather than outcomes is central to correcting mental accounting errors.

## Implications

Mental accounting leads to suboptimal financial decisions that can be mitigated through audits of how spending and income are categorized. Understanding mental accounting is important for both personal finance and policy design.
