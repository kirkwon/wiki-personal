# Two Views of Decision: Kahneman vs Klein

Daniel Kahneman and Gary Klein offer fundamentally different perspectives on intuitive decision-making, and their tension illuminates the central question of when intuition is reliable.

**Kahneman's view** (from *Thinking, Fast and Slow*):
- System 1 (intuitive) thinking is fast but error-prone, subject to cognitive biases.
- People systematically overestimate their ability to predict outcomes.
- Debiasing requires activating System 2 (analytical) thinking.
- The solution is awareness of biases and structured processes.

**Klein's view** (from *Sources of Power*):
- Expert intuition, built on extensive experience, is remarkably reliable in familiar domains.
- Experts recognize situations as patterns and mentally simulate responses—this is not irrational, it is the core of expert judgment.
- The problem is not intuition itself but the lack of experience or the mismatch between the situation and the expert's pattern library.
- The solution is building experience and designing systems that support natural thinking.

**The productive tension**:
- Kahneman is right that biases exist and can distort judgment, especially under uncertainty.
- Klein is right that expert intuition is powerful and that formal rationality models poorly capture real decision-making.
- The open question—when is intuition trustworthy and when are biases dominating—remains unresolved and is central to [[decision-making-under-uncertainty]] and [[decision-quality-vs-outcome-quality]].
