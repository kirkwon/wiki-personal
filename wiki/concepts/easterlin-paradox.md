# Easterlin Paradox

The Easterlin paradox is the observation that wealthier countries do not have higher self-reported happiness than poorer countries, and that across countries, wealthier nations do not report markedly higher happiness than poorer ones. Within a country, wealthier people tend to report higher life satisfaction than poorer people, yet as a country's income grows over time, its citizens' happiness does not increase proportionally. This challenges the assumption that more money equals more happiness and that economic growth automatically leads to greater well-being.

## Key Points

- Income above a basic threshold has diminishing returns on happiness
- Social comparison and relative income matter more than absolute income
- Rising GDP does not produce rising happiness over time in developed nations
- Basic needs (food, shelter, health) must be met before income has any effect on happiness
- People rapidly adjust to new circumstances and return to a baseline level of satisfaction—connecting to [[hedonic-adaptation]]

## Implications

Thaler uses this paradox to argue that beyond basic needs, pursuing additional income is unlikely to increase well-being. Understanding the psychology of happiness should inform both policy and personal decision-making. This connects to [[hedonic-adaptation]] and the broader economics of happiness literature.
