
# The Paradox of Change

The Paradox of Change is the central thesis of Atul Gawande's *Being Mortal*. It describes how modern healthcare's relentless drive to extend life creates a paradoxical situation: by trying to defeat aging and death at all costs, the medical system often undermines the very quality and meaning that make life worth living.

The paradox operates on multiple levels. Medicine has become extraordinarily powerful at curing diseases and prolonging biological life. Yet this capability generates a cultural expectation that every problem has a medical solution, and that more intervention is always better. The result is that many elderly patients undergo aggressive treatments — chemotherapy, ventilators, feeding tubes, ICU stays — not because these interventions improve their experience, but because the system defaults to "doing something" rather than asking what the patient actually values.

This mirrors the broader tension between [[quality-vs-quantity-of-life]]: extending the number of days lived does not necessarily extend the quality of those days. It also connects to [[overconfidence-and-certainty]] — medical professionals and families often overestimate what intervention can achieve and underestimate the suffering it causes.

Gawande argues the paradox is cultural as much as clinical. The medical profession was built on the premise that curing disease is the highest good. Palliative care and comfort-oriented approaches were historically marginalized as giving up. Reform requires not just new protocols but a fundamental shift in what medicine considers success.
