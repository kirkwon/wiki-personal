# Composition Techniques

Composition is how you arrange elements in your frame. Good composition makes your photos more visually appealing, guides the viewer's eye, and creates emotional impact. It is the primary driver of visual appeal in photography and is considered the foundational skill that separates snapshots from compelling images.

## Core Techniques

### Rule of Thirds
Divide your frame into a 3x3 grid (9 equal parts). Place important elements at the intersections of the grid lines, not in the center. This creates balance and natural tension, mimicking how humans naturally view scenes. Enable the grid on your camera, place the subject at intersections, and align the horizon with grid lines (top or bottom third, not middle). Practice placing subjects at intersections and aligning horizons with grid lines.

### Leading Lines
Lines in your image that guide the viewer's eye to your main subject. Types include straight lines (roads, fences — direct, strong), curved lines (rivers, paths — soft, flowing), diagonal lines (stairs, shadows — dynamic, energetic), and converging lines (railroad tracks — perspective, depth). Position yourself so lines lead to your subject and frame so lines start at the edge and go to the subject.

### Framing
Using elements in your scene to create a "frame" around your main subject. Types include natural frames (trees, arches, doorways), architectural frames (windows, door frames, arches), environmental frames (leaves, branches, rocks), and light frames (silhouettes, light beams). The frame doesn't need to be complete — partial framing works well.

### Directional Lighting in Composition
Golden hour provides directional light from the side or near the horizon, which is a powerful compositional tool. See [[golden-hour]] for how to use side lighting, front lighting, and backlighting to create depth and dimension. Look for long shadows and light patterns, use reflections (windows, water), and frame subjects with natural elements.

### Symmetry and Patterns
Symmetry uses mirrored elements for balance — vertical (reflections, architecture), horizontal (balanced top and bottom), or radial (from a center point). Patterns use repeating elements like windows, bricks, or leaves. Breaking a pattern by introducing one different element creates interest. Patterns can fill the frame as the subject, and breaking the pattern with one different element creates interest.

### Depth and Layers
Creating the illusion of 3D in a 2D medium through foreground elements (objects close to camera), background elements (distant mountains), leading lines, aerial perspective (distant objects less sharp, more blue), and multiple layers (foreground → middle ground → background).

### Negative Space
Empty space around your main subject that emphasizes the subject, creates breathing room, adds impact through minimalism, can suggest movement, and balances subject against negative space. Place the subject in one part of the frame and leave the rest empty.

### Golden Ratio
A mathematical ratio (1:1.618) found in nature and art. More complex than the rule of thirds. Apply via Fibonacci spiral (subject on spiral curve), golden triangle (subject at intersection of diagonal lines), or golden ratio grid. Useful for portraits (place eye on golden ratio point) and landscapes (horizon at golden ratio line). Similar to the rule of thirds but with different proportions, creating mathematical harmony in portraits, landscapes, and abstract work.

### Color Theory in Photography
Color harmony includes monochromatic (shades of one color — unified, calm), analogous (adjacent on color wheel — harmonious, natural), complementary (opposite on color wheel — high contrast, energetic), split complementary (balanced contrast), and triadic (three equally spaced colors — vibrant, balanced). Color temperature uses warm colors (red, orange, yellow) and cool colors (blue, green, purple) for visual interest through contrast.

### Perspective
Changing camera position creates different effects: eye level (natural, documentary), low angle (powerful, grand), high angle (diminished, overview), bird's eye (abstract, graphic), and worm's eye (dramatic, towering).

### Minimalism
Simplifying composition to essential elements by removing distractions, using simple backgrounds, focusing on one main subject, employing negative space, and using clean lines.

## Breaking the Rules

Rules should be learned first, then deliberately broken when the subject or effect demands it. Rules should be broken intentionally for effect — tension, imbalance, surprise, creative expression, or when the subject demands it. Examples include center composition for symmetry or dramatic effect, cluttered backgrounds for environmental portraits, no leading lines for flat graphic images, or sometimes the subject is better placed in the center. The key is intentional rule-breaking for creative expression or storytelling.

## Practice Exercises

- Daily: Practice rule of thirds, leading lines, and framing in every shot.
- Weekly: Master one technique per week, study master photographers' composition, practice breaking rules, and shoot the same scene from different perspectives.
- When viewing photos: Identify the main subject, trace eye movement, identify techniques used, notice balance, and consider color contribution.
- When creating photos: Identify the subject, choose the technique, frame carefully, review in camera, and take multiple shots varying composition.
- Study master photographers, analyze their composition, and take multiple shots varying composition to choose the best.

## Common Mistakes

- Subject in center → move to rule of thirds intersection
- Cluttered background → move to cleaner background or use wider aperture
- No focal point → have one clear subject, eliminate distractions
- Headroom too much/little → crop appropriately
- Crooked horizon → use grid, level camera
- Subject too small → get closer or use telephoto lens
