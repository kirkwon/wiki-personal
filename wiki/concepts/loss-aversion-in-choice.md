# Loss Aversion in Choice

The tendency to focus on foregone alternatives rather than the chosen option, increasing regret and reducing satisfaction after decisions. Loss aversion in choice contexts is a key driver of the paradox of choice.

## How It Works
When people evaluate options, they weigh potential losses more heavily than equivalent gains. After making a choice, the mind fixates on what was given up — the attractive features of the rejected alternatives. This asymmetry between chosen and unchosen options makes virtually any decision feel suboptimal.

## In Choice Overload
With many options available, the number of foregone alternatives increases dramatically. Each additional option considered adds to the pool of "lost" possibilities, intensifying post-decision regret regardless of how good the chosen option actually is.

## Relation to Prospect Theory
This concept extends [[prospect-theory]] (Kahneman & Tversky), which established that losses loom larger than gains in general evaluation. Loss aversion in choice applies this principle specifically to the domain of decision-making, where the comparison set expands with each option considered.

## Practical Implications
- Recognize that regret is amplified by the number of options considered, not just the quality of the chosen option
- Reducing the option set can paradoxically improve satisfaction
- Focus attention on the chosen option's positive attributes rather than dwelling on alternatives
