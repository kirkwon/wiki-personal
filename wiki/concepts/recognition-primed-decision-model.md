# Recognition-Primed Decision Model

The Recognition-Primed Decision (RPD) Model is [[gary-klein]]'s core theoretical contribution to understanding expert decision-making. Developed through years of field research with fireground commanders and other emergency professionals, RPD describes how experienced decision-makers size up situations, recognize patterns, and commit to actions — all without comparing alternative options in the traditional analytical sense.

## Overview

Unlike classical decision theory—which assumes people compare options and select the one with the highest expected value—RPD holds that experts rarely evaluate multiple options at all. The process works in three stages:

1.  **Situation recognition**: The decision-maker recognizes the situation as matching a familiar pattern from past experience. This pattern recognition happens almost instantly — a fireground commander sees smoke color, flame behavior, and building construction and recognizes "this is a basement fire spreading through the ductwork."
2.  **Response retrieval**: A typical response to that pattern is recalled from memory.
3.  **Mental simulation**: The response is mentally "run forward" to check for problems. If problems are detected — the water supply is insufficient, the roof is about to collapse — the plan is adjusted and re-simulated. If no problems emerge, the decision is acted upon.

This process takes seconds to minutes, not the hours implied by rational-choice models. Klein calls this rapid, unconscious recognition "intuition."

## Key Points

- Experts recognize situations by matching cues to patterns from prior experience.
- They evaluate courses of action through [[mental-simulation]], not option comparison.
- Typically only one course of action is considered at a time (serial, not parallel).
- The model explains fast, effective decision-making in time-pressured environments.
- Expertise is domain-specific — patterns only develop through thousands of hours of practice with clear feedback.
- The RPD model has been validated across firefighting, military command, nursing, chess, and business.

## Contrast with Other Frameworks

The RPD model challenges the assumption that good decisions require comparing multiple options. This is not a cognitive limitation but an adaptive strategy for time-pressured environments where generating and comparing options would be dangerous. The model also explains why novices struggle: they lack the repertoire of recognized patterns that experts draw on, so they must resort to slow, analytical reasoning that is both slower and more error-prone under pressure.

This contrasts with [[daniel-kahneman]]'s dual-process framework, which emphasizes the errors and biases of System 1 thinking. The tension between these views is explored in [[two-views-of-decision-kahneman-vs-klein]].
