# Social Norms vs. Market Norms

Dan Ariely's research distinguishes between two systems of human interaction: social norms and market norms. Under social norms, people behave based on relationships, reciprocity, and communal expectations — gifts are expected, helping is natural, and monetary compensation can feel insulting. Under market norms, transactions are governed by prices, contracts, and financial incentives. Social norms feature cooperation, reciprocation, and generosity, while market norms involve monetary transactions, cost-benefit analysis, and contractual obligations.

## Key Findings

- Introducing money into a social relationship can destroy the social bond and shift behavior to market norms
- People work harder and longer under social norms than when paid a small amount, because payment activates a market frame that lowers intrinsic motivation
- Gift-giving operates under social norms; charging for favors destroys the social contract
- Social norms can suppress efficient market reasoning
- Organizations that mix social and market norms create confusion and erode trust
- Paying volunteers or family members can backfire by replacing social norms with market norms

## Applications

- Designing organizational policies and incentive systems that don't inadvertently replace social norms with market norms
- Understanding why paying volunteers or family members can backfire
- Negotiating business deals where the boundary between social and market norms matters
- Recognizing when social pressures are influencing choices inappropriately

## When Social Norms Should Dominate

Ariely notes social norms legitimately should dominate in relationships, community contexts, and situations where emotional connections matter more than transactional efficiency.

## Implications

This distinction connects to [[trust-fundamentals]] — trust operates primarily under social norms, and monetizing relationships can corrode it. It also relates to [[skin-in-the-game]] because Taleb's accountability principle assumes a market-like consequence structure, while Ariely shows that social norms sometimes produce better outcomes without explicit incentives.

The concept challenges simple incentive design and raises questions for [[building-a-decision-culture]]: should organizations rely on social norms (culture, purpose) or market norms (pay, bonuses) to drive behavior? It also connects to [[ethical-design-manipulation-question]] and [[framing-effects]], as the frame — social or market — fundamentally changes how people respond to the same situation.
