
# Dishonesty Experiments

Dan Ariely's experimental research on dishonesty, including matrix task studies and MIT cheating experiments, which demonstrate that people cheat opportunistically but not maximally, and that small environmental changes dramatically alter cheating rates.

## Key Experiments

- **Matrix task experiments**: Participants were given matrices to solve and paid per correct answer, with opportunities to self-report results. Most cheated by a small margin rather than maximizing dishonest gains.
- **MIT cheating studies**: Showed that students cheat more when the incentive structure is ambiguous or when peers are also cheating.
- **Cognitive effort findings**: Neuroimaging revealed that lying activates additional brain regions compared to truth-telling, indicating that deception requires greater cognitive resources.

## Key Findings

- People are not either fully honest or fully dishonest; instead they occupy a "honorably dishonest" zone.
- Small changes in experimental conditions (e.g., distance from moral reminder, presence of peers) produce large changes in cheating rates.
- The self-image constraint is a more powerful limit on dishonesty than external punishment.

## Connection to Wider Research

These findings extend the [[cognitive-biases-library]] by identifying dishonesty-specific patterns: self-image preservation bias and situational cheating escalation. They complement [[dual-process-theory]] by showing that the cognitive effort of lying engages deliberate (System 2) processing.
