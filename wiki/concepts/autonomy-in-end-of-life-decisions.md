# Autonomy in End-of-Life Decisions

Autonomy in end-of-life decisions refers to the principle that patients should have genuine self-determination over their own care as they approach death. This includes the right to choose treatments, the right to refuse treatments, the right to define what makes life worth living, and the right to have those wishes respected by the medical system.

Gawande argues that autonomy is systematically undermined in practice. Patients are often not informed of their prognosis. Families are pressured to authorize interventions. The default assumption is that more treatment is better, making it difficult for patients to choose comfort or to stop treatment without feeling they are "giving up."

The concept connects to [[decision-quality-vs-outcome-quality]]: an autonomous decision — one made with full information and aligned with the patient's values — is a higher-quality decision even if the outcome is death sooner rather than later. It also connects to [[real-options-analysis]] because end-of-life choices can be treated as flexible — patients can shift goals as their condition changes, rather than committing irreversibly to a trajectory.

Respecting autonomy requires more than legal forms like advance directives. It requires a culture of honest conversation, where doctors and families can discuss what matters to the patient without equating openness with defeat.
