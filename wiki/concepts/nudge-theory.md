# Nudge Theory

[[nudge-theory]], introduced by Richard H. Thaler and Cass R. Sunstein, describes the practice of subtly influencing behavior through small environmental or contextual changes without restricting options or using incentives. A "nudge" is any aspect of the choice architecture that alters behavior predictably without forbidding options. It represents one pole of the [[choices]] mental model: rather than suffering from choice overload, people can be guided toward better outcomes through careful design.^[raw/articles/Choices.md]

## Core Ideas

- Traditional economic models assume rational agents; behavioral economics reveals systematic irrationalities that nudges can address
- Nudges are effective precisely because they preserve freedom of choice — people can still opt out
- **Choice architecture**: The way options are presented matters as much as the options themselves; it is the primary mechanism through which nudges are designed and deployed
- **Libertarian paternalism**: Influencing choices is acceptable as long as people remain free to choose
- **Defaults**: Setting a default option (e.g., organ donation opt-out) dramatically shifts behavior
- The concept draws on insights from [[cognitive-biases-library]] and [[dual-process-theory]], recognizing that people do not always act in their own stated best interests
- **Transparency**: Nudges should be easy to understand and not manipulative

## Applications

Nudges have been implemented in public policy across healthcare, education, and finance. For example, automatic enrollment in retirement savings plans, simplified opt-out procedures for organ donation, and redesigned default options in government forms. International governance models increasingly adopt behavioral insights, though challenges include unintended consequences and the risk of paternalism.

## Ethical Considerations

The moral implications of nudging center on the tension between improving welfare and respecting autonomy. [[persuasive-entities]] such as governments and corporations may deploy nudges for their own benefit rather than the chooser's, raising concerns about manipulation. This connects directly to the [[ethical-design-manipulation-question]] of whether designers optimize for engagement or user well-being.

## Key Tensions

- Nudge theory sits in tension with [[radical-transparency]] and [[skin-in-the-game]], which emphasize accountability and bearing consequences
- The line between helpful nudging and manipulative choice architecture is not clearly defined
- Public policy nudges must balance paternalism against the very autonomy they aim to support
- [[paradox-of-choice]]: Too many options can paralyze; nudges counteract this
