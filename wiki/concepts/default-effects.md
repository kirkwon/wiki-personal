# Default Effects

Default effects describe the phenomenon where people disproportionately stick with whatever option is pre-selected or presented as the default rather than actively choosing alternatives. This is closely related to the [[status-quo-bias]] and is a cornerstone of choice architecture and nudge theory, revealing how powerful defaults are in shaping behavior without any coercion. Default effects refer to the powerful tendency for people to stick with pre-selected options. When a choice is set as the default, a large majority of people accept it without actively opting out. This finding is one of the strongest empirical results in behavioral economics.

Defaults function as a form of implicit recommendation, signaling what the choice architect considers the preferred option, and the effect is amplified when opting out requires additional effort or attention. Default options can capture the majority of selections even when alternatives are equivalent.

## How Defaults Work

- People exhibit [[status-quo-bias]] — a preference for the current state of affairs
- When an option is pre-selected as the default, most individuals accept it without changing
- The effect is amplified when opting out requires additional effort or attention
- Default effects operate through status quo bias, endowment effect, and the cognitive effort required to switch from a default
- They connect to [[endowment-effect]] — people value what they already have

## Evidence and Examples

- Opt-out organ donation programs yield far higher participation rates than opt-in programs; when organ donation is opt-out rather than opt-in, registration rates increase dramatically
- Automatic enrollment in retirement savings plans increases participation rates dramatically compared to opt-in designs; retirement savings plans see higher enrollment when auto-enrollment is the default
- Pre-selected healthcare plans see higher adoption than those requiring active selection
- Pre-checked consent forms for organ donation or data sharing
- Default settings in software and consumer products

## Connection to Nudge Theory

Default effects are one of the most frequently deployed nudges and a primary mechanism through which nudges operate. Richard H. Thaler and Cass R. Sunstein emphasize that changing a default is often the simplest and most cost-effective way to improve outcomes at scale. By setting beneficial defaults, choice architects can improve aggregate outcomes while preserving individual freedom. This connects to [[choice-architecture]] and [[nudge-theory]] as core intervention tools. Because defaults are so influential, choosing the right default is a critical element of [[choice-architecture]] with direct policy applications in healthcare, education, and finance.
