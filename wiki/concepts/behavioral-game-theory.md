# Behavioral Game Theory

Behavioral game theory studies how people actually behave in strategic situations, challenging the predictions of standard (neoclassical) game theory. People cooperate more than expected, reject unfair offers, and care about fairness, reciprocity, and social norms in ways that standard models do not capture.

## Key Experiments

- **Ultimatum Game** – Responders routinely reject unfair offers (typically below 20% of the total; e.g., $2 out of $10) even at personal cost, contradicting the rational self-interest prediction and indicating that fairness concerns dominate pure self-interest.
- **Prisoner's Dilemma** – People cooperate at significantly higher rates than the Nash equilibrium prediction, especially in repeated interactions, suggesting that social preferences and reciprocity matter.
- **Fairness Norms** – Economic behavior is constrained by social norms that can override efficiency. People punish free-riders and unfair behavior even when punishment is costly, maintaining social order.

## Implications

Standard rational choice theory fails to predict actual human behavior in strategic contexts. Behavioral game theory provides more accurate models by incorporating fairness concerns, which connects to [[fairness-and-decision-making]] and [[fairness-and-social-norms]]. This challenges pure efficiency assumptions throughout economics and the [[decision-making-under-uncertainty]] framework based on pure rationality, showing that real decisions are shaped by social context, norms, and psychological factors.
