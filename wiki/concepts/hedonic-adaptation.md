# Hedonic Adaptation

Hedonic adaptation is the tendency for people to quickly return to a relatively stable level of happiness despite major positive or negative life events. When people receive windfalls, promotions, or favorable outcomes, their happiness increases temporarily but then reverts to baseline. After initial emotional reactions, people adjust to new circumstances and their baseline level of satisfaction reasserts itself.

## Key Findings

- Major life events (promotion, lottery win, disability) have less lasting impact on happiness than expected
- People adapt to both pleasant and unpleasant circumstances
- Sustained happiness is more influenced by ongoing conditions (relationships, work) than by one-time events
- Social comparison matters more than absolute income levels
- Additional income beyond basic needs has diminishing returns on happiness
- People treat windfalls differently from earned income, but adaptation reduces the lasting impact

This concept is central to Thaler's chapter on the economics of happiness and connects to the [[easterlin-paradox]]—the finding that income above basic needs has diminishing returns on happiness. It also relates to [[mental-accounting]], as people's happiness responses to windfalls and promotions are shaped by how they categorize and adapt to those gains.
