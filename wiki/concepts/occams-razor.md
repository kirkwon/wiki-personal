# Occam's Razor

Occam's Razor is a principle of parsimony attributed to William of Ockham, which states that among competing hypotheses, the one with the fewest assumptions should be selected. In practice, it is applied as a heuristic for choosing simpler explanations, solutions, or models when multiple options fit the available evidence.

The concept connects to decision-making frameworks and systematic approaches to problem-solving, where prioritizing simplicity helps avoid unnecessary complexity. It is relevant to productivity practices that aim to maximize output by eliminating redundant steps, tools, or processes.

This concept from *Super Thinking* encourages pruning unnecessary complexity and avoiding over-engineered solutions. While the source "Occam's Razor application" treats this as a productivity skill, the underlying principle is epistemological and has broader applications in science, philosophy, and everyday reasoning.

Related to [[bayesian-thinking]] in that simpler hypotheses often have higher prior probabilities in the absence of evidence.
