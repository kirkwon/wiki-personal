
# The Art of War - Sun Tzu

A classic Chinese military strategy text authored by Sun Tzu, comprising 13 chapters that cover planning, warfare costs, stratagem, positioning, energy, maneuvering, tactics variation, terrain, situations, fire attack, and espionage. It has become a foundational text for strategic thinking across domains and is referenced in the Positioning mental model as a cross-source perspective on understanding and navigating complex situations through pattern and relationship recognition.

## Core Principles
- The highest form of victory is winning without fighting through superior strategy
- Prolonged conflict is costly; rapid victory is preferable
- Positioning and defensive strength precede engagement
- Forces should be like water — adaptable, flowing to exploit weakness
- Intelligence gathering is essential; without understanding the enemy, success is unlikely
- Adaptability based on specific circumstances rather than rigid rules
- Deception and misdirection as strategic tools

## Five Factors and Seven Calculations
Sun Tzu's strategic assessment framework evaluates:
1. The moral law (unity of purpose)
2. Heaven (timing, weather)
3. Earth (terrain)
4. The commander (leadership qualities)
5. Method and discipline (organization)

## Relevance to Mental Models
Key themes of the text — strategic positioning, understanding the terrain of conflict, and the importance of situational awareness — align closely with the Positioning mental model's emphasis on mental shortcuts for complexity. The Art of War provides historical and philosophical grounding for positioning-based thinking, connecting to modern frameworks like [[strategic-vs-reactive-thinking]] and [[planning]].

## Connections to Decision-Making
This text extends [[decision-making-frameworks]] with ancient strategic principles emphasizing situational assessment before action. It connects to [[strategic-vs-reactive-thinking]] through its emphasis on proactive positioning and to [[infinite-game-mindset]] through long-term adaptability.

## Tension with Cooperative Contexts
The text acknowledges that the war metaphor can be inappropriate for cooperative contexts, and warns against treating every situation as zero-sum conflict — an open question for when strategic thinking becomes adversarial.
