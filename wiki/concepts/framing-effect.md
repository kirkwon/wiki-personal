# Framing Effect

The framing effect is the cognitive bias where the way information or choices are presented (framed) influences decisions, even when the underlying facts are identical. Richard Thaler's work highlights framing as a foundational concept in behavioral economics.

## Definition
People make different choices based on how equivalent options are described—emphasizing gains versus losses, or presenting information in different formats—rather than on the objective content of the information.

## Key Characteristics
- Equivalent information presented as a gain vs. a loss produces different choices.
- The format of presentation (percentage, absolute number, verbal description) changes perceived value.
- Contextual cues around a choice alter preference even without changing the choice itself.

## Examples
- "90% fat-free" vs. "10% fat" — same product, different perceived healthiness.
- "Survival rate: 95%" vs. "Mortality rate: 5%" — same medical outcome, different treatment choices.
- Investment returns framed as gains vs. losses changing risk tolerance.

## Connection to Choice Architecture
Understanding framing effects is essential to [[choice-architecture]] and [[nudge]] design. By recognizing how framing shapes decisions, designers can present choices in ways that lead to better outcomes without restricting freedom.
