# Scarcity Principle

One of Cialdini's six principles of influence. The scarcity principle holds that people assign more value to things that are less available or perceived as scarce. Limited availability of an object, idea, or offer increases its perceived value and desirability. People assign more worth to things that seem likely to run out.

## How It Works
Scarcity triggers a loss aversion response — the fear of missing out on something valuable. This urgency can override rational evaluation of the item or offer itself. Cialdini documents how scarcity increases perceived value through both quantity limits ("only 3 left in stock") and time pressure ("offer expires tonight"). The principle taps into loss aversion — people are more motivated to avoid losing something than to gain something equivalent. This connects to [[prospect-theory]], which describes how losses loom larger than gains in human psychology.

## Applications
- Creating urgency in sales and negotiations
- Understanding why "limited time" or "limited quantity" framing is effective
- Recognizing when scarcity is artificially manufactured to pressure decisions
- Scarcity also relates to [[variable-reward-design]] in product design, where unpredictable availability can drive engagement

## Connection to Existing Concepts
Scarcity connects to [[fear-of-missing-out-fomo]] and [[dread-aversion]]. It also relates to [[black-swan-events]] in the sense that perceived scarcity can distort risk assessment.

## Ethical Considerations
The principle carries ethical implications: artificial scarcity or manufactured urgency can constitute manipulation if it misleads consumers about genuine constraints.
