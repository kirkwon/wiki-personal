# Irrevocable Trusts

This page is part of the comprehensive [[trusts-guide]]. See also:

A trust that cannot be modified, amended, or terminated after creation without court approval or beneficiary consent. [[trusts-guide]] ^[raw/articles/Trusts Guide.md]

## Key Features

- **Irrevocability**: Once created, cannot be changed without consent
- **Asset Protection**: Generally protects assets from creditors
- **Tax Advantages**: Potential for income tax and estate tax benefits
- **Control Limitations**: Settlor loses direct control over assets
- **Perpetual Duration**: Can exist for multiple generations

## Common Uses

- Asset protection from creditors and lawsuits
- Estate tax planning to reduce liability
- Medicaid planning to qualify for benefits
- Providing for disabled beneficiaries
- Wealth transfer to future generations

## Types of Irrevocable Trusts

### Asset Protection Trusts
- Domestic Asset Protection Trust (DAPT)
- Offshore Asset Protection Trust
- Spendthrift Trust
- Self-Settled Trust

### Tax Planning Trusts
- Irrevocable Life Insurance Trust (ILIT)
- Qualified Personal Residence Trust (QPRT)
- Grantor Retained Annuity Trust (GRAT)
- Charitable Remainder Trust (CRT)
- Intentionally Defective Grantor Trust (IDGT)

### Special Purpose Trusts
- Special Needs Trust
- Dynasty Trust
- Spendthrift Trust
- Crummey Trust
- Pet Trust

## Key Trade-off

Irrevocable trusts provide significant benefits but require the settlor to give up direct control over assets. Professional guidance is necessary for compliance with federal and state law.
