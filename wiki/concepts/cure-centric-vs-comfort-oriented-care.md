# Cure-Centric vs. Comfort-Oriented Care

Gawande describes a fundamental divide in how medicine approaches suffering and decline. Cure-centric care seeks to defeat disease through aggressive intervention — surgery, chemotherapy, ICU management, ventilators. Comfort-oriented care seeks to relieve suffering and preserve quality of life, even when a cure is not possible.

The medical profession was historically built around the former. Palliative care was long considered a failure — a signal that doctors had given up. Gawande documents how this cultural bias persists: patients are offered aggressive treatment by default, and palliative conversations often only happen after the damage is done.

The shift Gawande advocates is not about abandoning treatment, but about redefining what treatment means. A conversation about what a patient values, a plan for managing pain, help with daily activities, and the space to say goodbye are all forms of care. They require the same skill, attention, and professionalism as a surgical intervention.

This connects to [[overconfidence-and-certainty]] — the belief that more intervention is always better mirrors cognitive overconfidence in medicine. It also underlies [[culture-change-in-healthcare]]: the shift requires changing not just protocols but the values and identity of the profession.
