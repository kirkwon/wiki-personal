# Return of Capital (ROC)

Return of Capital (ROC) is a distribution from an investment that is **not** income — it is a return of the investor's own money. Instead of being taxed as income immediately, ROC reduces your cost basis in the investment.

## What Is Return of Capital?

ROC is a non-taxable distribution that reduces your adjusted cost basis rather than being taxed as income. The distribution represents your own capital being returned to you, not earnings generated by the investment.

## How ROC Works

When a fund or security distributes more than its net investment income and realized gains, the excess is classified as ROC. This reduces the investor's adjusted cost basis:

```
Adjusted Cost Basis = Original Cost Basis - Total ROC Received
```

### Example
- Purchase: 1,000 shares at $20/share = $20,000 cost basis
- Year 1: $1,200 distribution, $800 is ROC → Basis = $19,200
- Year 2: $1,200 distribution, $600 is ROC → Basis = $18,600
- Year 3: $1,200 distribution, $400 is ROC → Basis = $18,200
- Total ROC received: $1,800
- Adjusted cost basis: $18,200

When cost basis reaches zero, additional ROC distributions are taxed as **long-term capital gains**.

## Tax Treatment

| Feature | Return of Capital | Ordinary Dividend | Qualified Dividend |
|---------|-------------------|-------------------|-------------------|
| Taxed in year received? | No (reduces basis) | Yes (ordinary income) | Yes (LTCG rates) |
| Tax rate | Deferred until sale | Up to 37% | 0%, 15%, or 20% |
| Impact on cost basis | Reduces basis | No impact | No impact |
| Reported on | Form 1099-DIV, Box 3 | Form 1099-DIV, Box 1a | Form 1099-DIV, Box 1b |
| Medicare surtax (3.8%) | No (deferred) | Yes | Yes |

## Where ROC Commonly Occurs

- **Closed-End Funds (CEFs)**: Often have 6-10%+ distribution rates with large ROC components. The trap is that unsuspecting investors equate high yield with high income, when in reality they may be eating into principal.
- **Covered Call ETFs**: QYLD, JEPI, NUSI frequently include ROC. Some use options premiums (income), others return principal.
- **REITs**: Must distribute 90%+ of taxable income. Depreciation creates paper losses, making ROC a significant portion of distributions. Depreciation recapture at sale applies a 25% rate on the depreciation portion.
- **MLPs (Master Limited Partnerships)**: Common in energy infrastructure (pipelines). Schedule K-1 tax reporting. ROC reduces basis and may create UBTI in tax-advantaged accounts.
- **Bond Funds**: Selling bonds at a loss, returning principal from maturing bonds, or amortizing bond premiums.

## ROC Tax Deferral Advantage

ROC can be powerful in taxable accounts:
- No tax until you sell the investment
- Control timing of gain realization (e.g., in lower tax bracket years)
- Compounding effect: deferred taxes mean more money compounding
- Reinvestment has no tax drag

### When ROC is a Disadvantage
- Eroding principal: true ROC means your investment value decreases
- Tracking complexity: must track adjusted basis for accurate tax reporting
- Sale at loss: if you sell below adjusted basis, loss deduction is smaller
- Hidden in yield: high-yield investments may be mostly returning your own money

## Strategic Uses of ROC

1. **Tax-Efficient Income in Retirement**: ROC provides cash flow without immediate tax, ideal for retirees in high tax brackets.
2. **Tax Bracket Management**: Receive ROC in high-income years (no tax), sell shares in low-income years (LTCG rates).
3. **Reinvestment Compounding**: Reinvest ROC distributions — no tax drag on reinvested ROC.

## ROC in Different Account Types

### Taxable Accounts
- ROC provides tax deferral benefit
- **Best use case** for ROC strategy

### Traditional IRA / 401(k)
- ROC has NO tax benefit — everything taxed as ordinary income on withdrawal
- Prefer funds with ordinary income (dividends) in tax-deferred accounts

### Roth IRA
- No tax on anything → ROC irrelevant
- Prefer highest total return regardless of distribution type
- Prefer growth-oriented funds in Roth accounts

## Reporting

ROC is reported on Form 1099-DIV, Box 3 (Nondividend Distributions). Most brokers track adjusted cost basis, but investors should verify annually and keep their own records as backup.

## Quick Decision Framework

**Should You Care About ROC?**
- **Yes** if: investing in taxable accounts, high tax bracket, income-focused strategy
- **No** if: investing only in tax-advantaged accounts, long-term growth focus
- **Maybe** if: mix of account types, moderate tax bracket

**Evaluating a Fund's ROC**:
1. Check distribution breakdown: Income vs ROC vs capital gains
2. Calculate true yield: Only count income portion
3. Assess NAV trend: Is ROC eroding principal?
4. Consider tax bracket: Does deferral benefit you?
5. Compare alternatives: Is there a more tax-efficient option?
