
# Deliberate Practice

Structured, effortful practice focused on improving performance in any field. Distinct from passive repetition, entertainment, or routine work. Covered in Part VII of *Super Thinking* and central to Cal Newport's argument that mastery — and by extension career satisfaction — comes from developing rare skills rather than chasing pre-existing passion. Cal Newport emphasizes deliberate practice as the mechanism for building **career capital**—the rare and valuable skills that make you indispensable.

## Definition
Deliberate practice is a method of practice characterized by:
- **Focused effort** on specific weaknesses or challenging aspects of performance
- **Structured feedback** loops that identify errors and guide improvement
- **Progressive difficulty** — constantly pushing just beyond current ability
- **Repetition with variation** — practicing the same skill in different contexts to build flexibility

## Core Idea
- Practice with clear goals and immediate feedback.
- Operate at the edge of your ability — in the zone between comfort and frustration.
- Focus on weaknesses, not strengths.
- Seek feedback and adjust accordingly.

## Origin
Based on Anders Ericsson's research on expert performance. Newport repurposes this concept for career development, arguing that the same principles apply to professional skill building.

## Key Distinctions
- **Not passive repetition**: Simply doing the same task repeatedly does not constitute deliberate practice. Simply doing something repeatedly (e.g., playing golf every weekend) does not constitute deliberate practice unless you are specifically working on identified weaknesses with feedback.
- **Not entertainment**: Activities that feel enjoyable but don't stretch your abilities are not deliberate practice.
- **Not routine work**: Standard job tasks, even when performed well, typically don't provide the focused challenge needed for improvement.

## Connection to Other Concepts
Weinberg groups deliberate practice with [[spaced-repetition]] and the [[feynman-technique]] as learning tools. It connects to [[growth-mindset]] — the belief that abilities can be developed through effort. Relates to [[active-revision-techniques]] for structured learning, [[feedback-loops-in-skill-development]] for the role of criticism, and [[teaching-problem-solving]] for transferring expertise. Also connected to [[spaced-repetition]] and [[feynman-technique]] as part of the learning toolkit. Contrast with [[hook-model]] and [[internal-triggers]] — Newport is skeptical of passion-driven habit formation as a substitute for skill development. Deliberate practice connects to [[career-capital]] and [[career-mental-model]] as the mechanism for building rare and valuable skills. It also relates to [[cal-newport]], [[hyperfocus]], and [[deep-work---cal-newport]], which describe the kind of sustained attention required for effective deliberate practice.

## Tension with Passion-First Frameworks
Newport argues that passion is a result of skill development, not a prerequisite. This contrasts with frameworks like [[grit-concept]] (Duckworth) that emphasize sustained passion, and [[hooked---nir-eyal]] that treats internal emotional triggers as drivers of engagement.
