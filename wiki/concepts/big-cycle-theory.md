# Big Cycle Theory

Big Cycle Theory is Ray Dalio's framework for understanding the long-term rise and decline of empires, nations, and economic systems. It identifies recurring patterns driven by debt accumulation, monetary policy choices, internal cohesion, and shifts in global power.

## Core Ideas

- Empires and economies follow cyclical patterns of ascent and decline over decades or centuries.
- Debt cycles are central: prolonged borrowing finances growth but eventually creates vulnerabilities.
- Monetary policy divergence between nations accelerates power transitions.
- Internal political cohesion and governance quality determine how well a nation manages its cycle.
- New powers rise as existing ones overextend or decline.

## Application

Dalio applies this framework to the current moment, arguing that the United States and Western economies are in a late-cycle phase while China and other Eastern nations are in ascendant phases. This analysis connects to [[scenario-planning]] by providing a structural lens for modeling geopolitical futures, and to [[decision-making-under-uncertainty]] by suggesting that awareness of cyclical patterns improves strategic positioning.

## Tensions

The cyclical, somewhat deterministic nature of Big Cycle Theory contrasts with [[antifragility]] and [[black-swan-events]], which emphasize radical unpredictability and systems that improve under stress rather than following predictable arcs.
