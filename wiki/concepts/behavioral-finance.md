# Behavioral Finance

Behavioral finance examines how psychological factors—personal history, ego, cognitive biases, and emotional state—shape financial decisions more than technical knowledge or intelligence. The central insight is that financial success is driven by behavior and mindset rather than investment skill or market timing.

## Core Principles

**Personal history shapes money scripts.** Your economic upbringing creates deeply held beliefs about money. Depression-era grandparents instill extreme frugality; tech boom survivors develop speculation tolerance; housing crisis victims become real estate skeptics. What seems "obvious" financially is deeply personal and unique to each individual.

**Luck and risk are inseparable.** Success equals hard work plus luck; failure equals poor decisions plus risk. Bill Gates was extremely smart AND lucky—born in 1955 with access to early computer resources. The Vietnam War draft showed how birth month alone determined life outcomes. It is extremely difficult to distinguish between the two after the fact.

**Getting wealthy and staying wealthy require different mindsets.** Getting wealthy demands taking risks, being optimistic, and leveraging opportunities. Staying wealthy requires managing risk, being pessimistic, preserving capital, and being paranoid about losing it. "Money is most often made by concentrating, but kept by diversifying."

**Reasonable decisions beat rational ones for sustainability.** A mathematically optimal decision (paying off low-interest debt with cash) may be emotionally unsustainable if it leaves you without an emergency fund. Making decisions you can live with emotionally—what this framework calls "reasonable" rather than "rational"—produces better long-term outcomes because it avoids spillover costs from stress.

## Key Behavioral Biases in Finance

- [[hindsight-bias]] — Believing past events were more predictable than they were
- [[confirmation-bias]] — Seeking information that confirms existing beliefs
- [[loss-aversion]] — Losses hurt more than equivalent gains feel good
- [[anchoring]] — Fixating on irrelevant reference points for valuation
- [[survivorship-bias]] — Focusing on winners while ignoring losers

## Connection to Decision Quality

This framework connects to [[decision-quality-vs-outcome-quality]] by emphasizing that outcomes are poor judges of decision quality. A good process combined with bad luck is not a bad decision, and vice versa.
