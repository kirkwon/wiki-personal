# Prospect Theory

## Overview

Prospect Theory is a behavioral model of decision-making under risk, developed by [[daniel-kahneman]] and [[amos-tversky]] in 1979. It was the work that launched behavioral economics and earned [[daniel-kahneman]] the Nobel Prize. The theory replaced classical expected utility theory as a descriptive model of how people actually make risky choices — as opposed to how rational agents *should* make them.

The theory has two core components. First, the **value function** is S-shaped: concave for gains (risk-averse behavior) and convex for losses (risk-seeking behavior), and importantly, it is steeper for losses than for gains. This asymmetry — captured in the concept of [[loss-aversion]] — means that a loss of $X hurts about twice as much as a gain of $X feels good. Second, the **probability weighting function** shows that people overweight small probabilities and underweight moderate-to-large probabilities, which explains why people simultaneously buy lottery tickets (overweighting tiny win probabilities) and buy insurance (overweighting tiny loss probabilities).

Prospect Theory accounts for a wide range of empirical findings that classical economics cannot explain: the endowment effect (people value what they own more than identical items they don't own), the disposition effect (investors sell winners too early and hold losers too long), and framing effects (the same choice presented as a gain vs. a loss produces opposite preferences). These effects are central to the [[cognitive-biases-library]] and have practical implications for marketing, public policy, and personal finance.

## Key Points

- Value function: concave for gains, convex for losses — diminishing sensitivity in both directions
- Loss aversion: losses loom larger than equivalent gains (ratio ~2:1)
- Probability weighting: small probabilities are overweighted, medium/large probabilities are underweighted
- Reference dependence: outcomes are evaluated relative to a reference point, not in absolute terms
- Framing effects: the same objective choice, presented differently, can reverse preferences
- Cumulative Prospect Theory (1992) extended the model to handle multiple-outcome gambles
- Dread aversion: a form of loss aversion related to the fear of potential losses
- Kelly criterion and expected value: practical tools connected to decision-making under uncertainty
