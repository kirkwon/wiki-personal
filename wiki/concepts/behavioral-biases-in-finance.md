# Behavioral Biases in Finance

Financial decisions are systematically distorted by cognitive biases. Understanding these biases is essential for making better financial choices, since technical knowledge alone does not protect against poor psychology.

## Key Biases

### Hindsight Bias
Believing past events were more predictable than they actually were. This leads to overconfidence in future predictions and a false sense that markets are more predictable than they are. The solution is to actively remember the uncertainty that existed at the time of past decisions.^[raw/articles/The Psychology of Money.md]

### Confirmation Bias
Seeking information that confirms existing beliefs while filtering out disconfirming evidence. In finance, this creates filter bubbles where investors only read news that supports their current positions, leading to poor diversification and missed warning signs.^[raw/articles/The Psychology of Money.md]

### Loss Aversion
Losses hurt more than equivalent gains feel good. This asymmetry causes overly conservative decisions, such as holding losing positions too long or selling winners too early. Framing decisions appropriately can help mitigate this bias.^[raw/articles/The Psychology of Money.md]

### Anchoring
Fixating on irrelevant reference points, such as a stock's previous high price or a home's original purchase price. This leads to poor valuation decisions. Using multiple reference points rather than a single anchor helps counteract this tendency.^[raw/articles/The Psychology of Money.md]

### Survivorship Bias
Focusing on winners while ignoring losers. In finance, this means overestimating the success rates of strategies by only looking at their survivors. Looking at the whole population — including failed funds, bankrupt companies, and abandoned strategies — provides a more accurate picture.^[raw/articles/The Psychology of Money.md]

## Connection to Broader Bias Research

These biases connect to [[cognitive-biases-library]] and [[thinking-fast-and-slow---daniel-kahneman]], which provide a more comprehensive taxonomy of cognitive biases. Prospect theory [[prospect-theory]] specifically models loss aversion and the asymmetric valuation of gains and losses.

## Practical Implications

Recognizing these biases allows investors to separate decision quality from outcome quality [[decision-quality-vs-outcome-quality]]. A good process can still produce a bad outcome due to bad luck, and a bad process can still produce a good outcome due to good luck.^[raw/articles/The Psychology of Money.md]

The key is to focus on process rather than outcomes — you can control your decisions but not the results.^[raw/articles/The Psychology of Money.md]
