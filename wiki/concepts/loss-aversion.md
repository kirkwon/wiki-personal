# Loss Aversion

Loss aversion is the tendency for losses to loom larger than equivalent gains. People experience the pain of losing a given amount as significantly more intense than the pleasure of gaining the same amount. A loss of $100 creates roughly twice the psychological impact of gaining $100. This asymmetry fundamentally shapes risk-taking behavior, risk avoidance, and decision-making under uncertainty.

Kahneman and Tversky's prospect theory formalized loss aversion as a central principle, showing that the value function is steeper for losses than for gains. This principle explains why people hold losing investments too long, avoid necessary risks, and react disproportionately to threats.

## Key Characteristics

- Losses feel roughly twice as powerful as equivalent gains
- Drives risk-averse behavior in gains frame and risk-seeking in losses frame
- Reference point dependent — losses are measured relative to a status quo
- Affects financial decisions, negotiations, and everyday choices
- Investors demand higher returns for risky assets than expected utility theory predicts
- People are reluctant to accept fair bets

## Practical Implications

Loss aversion explains the endowment effect (ownership creates a reference point), status quo bias, and the tendency to overpay to avoid a sure loss. It is central to understanding [[framing-effects-in-decision-making]] — the same outcome framed as a loss triggers different behavior than when framed as a gain. Understanding loss aversion is essential for [[decision-making-frameworks]] and helps explain many counterintuitive behaviors in finance, negotiations, and everyday choices.

## Interactions with Other Biases

Loss aversion interacts with [[dread-aversion]] — the anticipation of future losses creates additional emotional weight beyond the loss itself. It also connects to [[endowment-effect]], where people assign higher value to things they already own simply because the prospect of losing them feels more painful than the pleasure of gaining them.
