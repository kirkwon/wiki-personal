# Conjecture and Criticism

Conjecture and criticism is David Deutsch's epistemological framework for how knowledge grows. According to Deutsch, knowledge advances not through observation, induction, or accumulation of data, but through the active generation of conjectures (possible explanations) and the critical refutation of errors in those conjectures.

## Core Mechanism

1. **Conjecture**: Generate a new explanation or hypothesis for some phenomenon.
2. **Criticism**: Attempt to refute it through logical analysis, empirical testing, thought experiments, or comparison with existing knowledge.
3. **Error elimination / Retention or rejection**: If criticisms succeed, the conjecture is abandoned or revised. If it survives criticism, it remains provisionally accepted; if successful, it is replaced by a better conjecture.
4. **Iteration**: The process repeats, generating increasingly powerful explanations. The cycle is continuous and self-correcting.

## Key Principles

- Knowledge grows by eliminating errors, not by confirming truths.
- No amount of evidence can prove a theory true (the problem of induction).
- A theory's value lies in what it excludes and how much it explains.
- Criticism must be constructive — aimed at error-correction rather than mere disagreement.
- Conjectures are evaluated partly by how much explanatory reach they possess.

## Relationship to Problem-Solving

Deutsch frames problems as symptoms of insufficient knowledge. The conjecture-and-criticism cycle is the method for creating the knowledge needed to solve them. This connects to [[problem-solving]] by reframing obstacles as knowledge gaps rather than impossible barriers.

## Practical Application

When facing a problem, Deutsch's approach suggests:
- Frame the problem as a knowledge gap.
- Generate multiple conjectures (explanations) for why the problem exists.
- Critically test each conjecture for errors.
- Retain explanations that survive criticism and use them to create new knowledge.

## Connection to Decision-Making

This framework relates to [[decision-making-frameworks]] and [[decision-quality-vs-outcome-quality]] by emphasizing that the quality of a decision process matters more than the outcome. A good decision is one made through rigorous conjecture and criticism, even if the outcome is unfavorable. Recording and reviewing decisions over time — [[decision-tracking]] — helps calibrate judgment. Reframing failure as a catalyst for growth — [[embracing-failure-for-personal-growth]] — is also central to this mindset.

## Contrast with Inductivism and Bayesian Thinking

Deutsch explicitly rejects inductivism — the idea that knowledge grows by accumulating observations and drawing generalizations. Unlike inductive reasoning, which generalizes from observed cases, conjecture and criticism does not require enumerating instances. It relies on explanatory power and the ability to withstand criticism. This positions it in tension with [[bayesian-thinking]], which updates beliefs based on accumulated evidence.
