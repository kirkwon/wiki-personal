# California Property Tax Transfer Rules

Proposition 19, effective February 16, 2021, significantly modified California's property tax transfer rules under Proposition 13. The changes affect inherited property, parent-child transfers, disabled individuals, and generate wildfire prevention funding estimated at $750-1 billion over 10 years.

## Key Changes from Proposition 13

Under Proposition 13 (1978), base year value was preserved on transfers between parent-child, disability transfers, or inherited property with no reassessment. Proposition 19 narrowed these protections significantly.

### Parent-to-Child Transfers

**Full Protection Qualifies:**
- Primary residence transfers receive full tax basis protection
- Family farms valued at $1 million or less receive full protection
- Severely disabled children always receive full protection regardless of property type

**Partial/No Protection:**
- Non-qualified properties are reassessed at current market value
- The difference between old and new base year value becomes subject to property tax
- Capital gains basis (original purchase price) is still inherited for sale calculations

**Before vs. After Impact:**
A property purchased in 1980 at $100,000 with current market value of $1,000,000 would see annual property tax rise from approximately $1,000 to $10,000+ on a non-qualified transfer — an increase of $9,000+ annually.

### Inherited Property Transfers

- Primary residences can retain basis if the inheriting child keeps it as their primary residence
- Other properties are generally reassessed at market value
- Children under 22 must use the property as primary residence to qualify
- Transfer must occur within the same county to maintain full protection

### Severely Disabled Individuals

- Transfers to severely disabled individuals always receive full property tax basis protection
- No property type restrictions apply
- Requires medical certification of disability status
- Protection is permanent regardless of future changes in disability status

### Senior, Veteran, and Disabled Homeowner Transfers

Seniors (55+), disabled individuals, and wildfire victims can transfer property tax basis to a replacement property of equal or lesser value within the same county. Key features:
- Unlimited number of transfers (unlike previous Proposition 60/90)
- Replacement property value must be ≤ sale price of original property
- Can transfer to any property type (single-family, condo, etc.)

### Wildfire Victims

- Properties damaged or destroyed by wildfires receive reassessment relief
- Can transfer property tax basis to replacement property in same county
- Must replace property within 2 years of wildfire declaration
- Requires proof of property damage from fire authority

## Strategic Considerations

### Tax Basis vs. Property Tax Tradeoff

Lower property taxes on inherited property may mean higher capital gains taxes at sale. The interaction with [[1031-exchange-strategy]] may make deferral preferable to inheritance protection in some cases. Longer holding periods mitigate tax increases.

### Trust Structure Interactions

[[trusts-guide]] notes that pour-over trusts, bypass trusts, and generation-skipping trusts have complex interactions with Proposition 19. Modified trust strategies with residential trust provisions and timing clauses may be necessary. Consult [[irrevocable-trusts]] and [[revocable-living-trusts]] for structure-specific considerations.

### County-Specific Implementation

Different counties implement Proposition 19 rules with varying interpretations. County location has become more critical for transfer planning. Replacement properties must be in the same county for senior transfer protections.

## Documentation Requirements

Required documentation includes birth certificates for parent-child transfers, medical certification for disability transfers, proof of primary residence (utility bills, voter registration, tax returns), and county residency documentation. Updated trust agreements and properly executed transfer documents are essential.

## Open Questions

- Ongoing legal challenges and legislative modifications may change specific provisions
- County implementation varies, creating uncertainty in planning
- Federal interaction with estate tax and capital gains rules remains an evolving area
