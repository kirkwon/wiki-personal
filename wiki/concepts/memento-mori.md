# Memento Mori

Memento mori — "remember that you will die" — is a Stoic practice of reflecting on mortality as a tool for valuing time and living purposefully. Seneca emphasizes this in *Letters from a Stoic* as a means to avoid wasting life on trivial pursuits and to maintain focus on what truly matters. Seneca explores this concept in *Letters from a Stoic*, Chapter 4, and employs it throughout *On the Shortness of Life* to urge readers to stop postponing happiness and purpose.

## Key Ideas

- Mortality reflection serves as a counterweight to complacency and distraction.
- Mortality reflection is not morbid but clarifying: it strips away distractions and false priorities.
- It should motivate purposeful action rather than paralysis or avoidance of living fully.
- Fear of death often stems from irrational assumptions about what happens after death.
- Regular contemplation of death helps maintain urgency and intentionality in daily life.
- The practice must be balanced: using mortality awareness to energize life, not to withdraw from it.
- Connects to [[balancing-ambition-with-well-being]] by redirecting focus from external achievements to internal virtue.
- Death awareness practices clarify what truly matters, support legacy and impact planning, and enable regular assessment of whether you are living or merely existing.

## Practice

Seneca recommends not dwelling obsessively on death, but periodically turning attention to it as a check against complacency. This practice supports [[quadrant-2-focus]] by ensuring time is spent on what is truly important rather than urgent but trivial matters. It also connects to present-moment awareness practices found in modern mindfulness and [[present-moment-living]].

## Tensions and Boundaries

Seneca warns against using mortality reflection to avoid future planning or to create anxiety about death. The practice should motivate urgency, not paralysis. Similarly, "living in the present" should not become avoidance of necessary future planning.

## When to Stop

When mortality reflection becomes a mechanism for avoiding fully engaging with life — withdrawing from society, suppressing joy, or using death anxiety as an excuse for inaction.
