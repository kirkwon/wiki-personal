# Satisfaction Under Constraints

The counterintuitive finding that people often report higher satisfaction when their options are limited compared to when they have abundant choices. This challenges the assumption that more freedom and more options always lead to greater happiness.

## Core Finding
Satisfaction is not maximized by having more options — it is often higher when constraints limit the choice set.^[The Paradox of Choice - Barry Schwartz.md]

## Key Insight
Satisfaction is not a simple function of the objective quality of the chosen option. It is heavily influenced by the perceived quality of the choice set and the psychological burden of selection. When options are scarce, people lower their expectations, feel less regret, and report higher satisfaction.

## Why This Happens
- Fewer options reduce the cognitive burden of evaluation.
- Limited options reduce the number of forgone alternatives, decreasing regret.
- Constraints create a sense of sufficiency that abundance cannot provide.
- [[prospect-theory]] — loss aversion amplifies regret under abundance.

## Evidence
Research comparing satisfaction levels across different choice environments shows that participants with fewer options consistently rate their decisions more favorably than those with many options, even when the objectively available options are identical.

## Tension with Optimization
This concept creates tension with frameworks like [[expected-value-ev]] and [[decision-tracking]] that emphasize thorough evaluation and maximizing outcomes. Schwartz would argue that the process of evaluating many options degrades satisfaction regardless of outcome quality — the optimization itself is the burden.

## Practical Takeaway
Sometimes limiting your options deliberately — by setting boundaries, trusting defaults, or relying on heuristics — produces better subjective outcomes than exhaustive analysis. Deliberately imposing limits on options can improve both decision speed and satisfaction.^[The Paradox of Choice - Barry Schwartz.md]
