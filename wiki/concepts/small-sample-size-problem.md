# Small Sample Size Problem

The small sample size problem refers to the tendency to draw misleading conclusions about cause and effect relationships when working with insufficient data. Random events can have a disproportionate impact on our understanding when sample sizes are small, leading us to see patterns or causation where none exists. Small samples are particularly vulnerable to being shaped by a few extreme or random outcomes, producing false cause-and-effect relationships.

This is not merely a statistical error but a deeply human cognitive failure — we naturally construct narratives from limited evidence, turning random sequences into stories with causal explanations. Taleb emphasizes that in financial markets and everyday decision-making, small sample sizes create an illusion of understanding that masks underlying randomness.

## Definition
When we observe only a few data points or experiences, we are prone to overgeneralizing. A small sample can make a random event appear systematic or causal, distorting our perception of reality.

## Practical Implications
- Be skeptical of causal claims derived from limited observations
- Recognize that success in a small number of trials may reflect randomness rather than skill
- Use larger samples when possible, or explicitly acknowledge uncertainty
- [[decision-tracking]] is valuable — tracking many decisions over time provides a larger sample for calibrating judgment
