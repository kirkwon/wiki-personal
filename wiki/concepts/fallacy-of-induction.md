# Fallacy of Induction

The fallacy of induction is the error of assuming that because something has happened repeatedly in the past, it will continue to happen in the future — particularly in systems governed by randomness. Taleb argues that while historical data may provide some insight, it cannot guarantee accuracy in forecasting random occurrences. This challenges the foundation of many decision-making approaches that assume the future will resemble the past.

## Definition
Induction assumes that because something has happened repeatedly in the past, it will continue to happen in the future. Taleb argues this is fundamentally flawed when dealing with random or uncertain systems.

## Overview

Relying solely on past experiences to predict future events is unreliable when dealing with random occurrences. Historical data may provide some insight, but it cannot guarantee accuracy in forecasting, especially in domains where randomness plays a dominant role.

Taleb's position is that induction is fundamentally fragile under deep uncertainty — the assumption that "what happened before will happen again" breaks down precisely when it matters most, in the face of novel or extreme events.

## Connection to Taleb's Work
This fallacy is central to *Fooled by Randomness*. It connects to the broader critique that [[black-swan-events]] — rare, unpredictable events — cannot be forecasted from past data patterns.

## Tension with Existing Frameworks

This concept challenges the wiki's reliance on historical frameworks:
- [[scenario-planning]] constructs future scenarios based on historical trends and relies heavily on past experience and pattern recognition
- [[monte-carlo-simulations]] use historical distributions to model future outcomes
- [[decision-making-frameworks]] often reference past case studies for guidance
- [[naturalistic-decision-making]] relies heavily on past experience and pattern recognition

This concept directly tensions with [[Superforecasting - Philip E Tetlock  Dan Gardner]], which shows that structured prediction methods can improve accuracy. Taleb's position suggests these approaches have fundamental limits when randomness dominates.
