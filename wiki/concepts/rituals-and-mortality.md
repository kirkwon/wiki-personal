# Rituals and Mortality

Gawande identifies rituals, routines, and symbolic gestures as essential tools for coping with mortality and preserving meaning during the final stages of life. These are not merely religious or cultural formalities — they are practical mechanisms through which people process loss, maintain connection, and find coherence in the face of death.

Rituals can take many forms: a family gathering to share memories, a ceremonial goodbye, the act of preparing a favorite meal, or even the small daily routines that give structure to a day when everything else is uncertain. They serve a psychological function by creating moments of intentionality and presence at a time when life feels out of control.

Gawande draws on examples from different cultural contexts to show how communities have historically supported dying through structured practices. The modern medical system, by contrast, often strips away these supports in favor of clinical routines. The result is a death that is medically managed but personally hollow.

This concept connects to [[the-good-death]] — rituals are a vehicle for dignity and family involvement. It also relates to [[autonomy-in-end-of-life-decisions]] because patients who can express their wishes about how they want to be remembered are better able to shape their final experience.
