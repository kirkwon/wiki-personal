# Being Mortal - Atul Gawande

Atul Gawande's *Being Mortal* examines how modern healthcare systematically conflicts with what makes life meaningful as people age. The book argues that the medical system is organized around curing disease and extending life at all costs, which often ignores patient autonomy, comfort, dignity, and the quality of one's final experiences.

## Core Concepts

- [[the-paradox-of-change]] — Modern medicine extends life aggressively but often ignores what makes life meaningful.
- [[the-good-death]] — Autonomy, comfort, dignity, and family involvement in dying.
- [[quality-vs-quantity-of-life]] — The tension between extending lifespan and preserving quality.
- [[cure-centric-vs-comfort-oriented-care]] — Shift from relentless intervention to palliative values.
- [[rituals-and-mortality]] — Symbolic practices that give meaning to dying and bereavement.
- [[the-costs-of-care]] — Economic burden of end-of-life medical expenditure.
- [[culture-change-in-healthcare]] — Need for systemic reform in how medicine treats aging and death.
- [[autonomy-in-end-of-life-decisions]] — Patient self-determination over medical protocols.

## Key Arguments

The central claim is that the modern healthcare system prioritizes cure over comfort, extending life regardless of quality. Gawande draws on clinical cases, conversations with patients and families, and comparative examples (nursing homes, palliative care programs) to argue that time spent on interventions that don't improve quality of life is often worse than time spent in comfort and connection. The "gift of time" reframes medical success away from survival metrics toward experiential metrics.
