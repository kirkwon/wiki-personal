# Range - David Epstein

David Epstein's *Range: Why Generalists Triumph in a Specialized World* argues that breadth of experience and cross-domain thinking lead to better outcomes than narrow specialization. The book supports the [[fields]] mental model by demonstrating how drawing from multiple fields enables pattern recognition and creative problem-solving. Epstein challenges the assumption that early specialization leads to success, arguing that diverse learning, analogical thinking, and maintaining range is more valuable than narrow expertise, especially in complex, changing domains.

## Key Themes

- Breadth of knowledge as advantage
- Cross-domain pattern recognition
- Value of range in complex situations
- The value of generalism and interdisciplinary thinking
- How diverse experience creates flexible expertise
- Pattern recognition across domains

## Key Chapters and Ideas

- **The Cult of the Head Start** – Late bloomers and generalists often outperform early specialists.
- **How the Wicked World Was Made** – Distinguishes "kind" learning environments (repeating patterns, clear feedback) from "wicked" ones (shifting patterns, ambiguous feedback), where breadth matters most.
- **When Less of the Same Is More** – Overlearning and narrow training can reduce adaptability; variety improves transfer.
- **Thinking Outside Experience** – Analogical thinking across distant domains drives creativity and innovation.
- **The Trouble with Too Much Grit** – Perseverance can become counterproductive when switching paths is optimal.
- **The Outsider Advantage** – Cross-disciplinary outsiders disrupt fields unburdened by domain-specific assumptions.
- **Lateral Thinking with Withered Technology** – Combining existing knowledge in new configurations drives innovation.
- **Fooled by Expertise** – Specialists can be misled in complex, changing systems; generalists often outperform.
- **Polymaths and the Modern World** – Increasing problem complexity requires generalist thinkers who connect ideas across domains.
