# Return of Capital (ROC)

A distribution from an investment that returns the investor's own capital rather than generating income. ROC reduces cost basis instead of being taxed as income, providing tax deferral benefits but potentially eroding principal if not tracked carefully.

## Key Concepts

- **Mechanism**: ROC reduces adjusted cost basis rather than being taxed as income. When cost basis reaches zero, additional ROC is taxed as long-term capital gains.
- **ROC vs ordinary dividends vs capital gains distributions**
- **Tax Treatment**: ROC is reported on Form 1099-DIV (Box 3) and is non-taxable in the year received. It defers tax until the investment is sold.
- **Tax deferral in taxable accounts**
- **Yield trap**: High headline yield masking principal erosion. Example: a 10% yield with 60% ROC provides only 4% true income.
- **Cost basis reduction mechanism**
- **Form 1099-DIV reporting (Box 3)**

## Common Scenarios

- Closed-End Funds (CEFs) with high distribution rates
- Covered Call ETFs (QYLD, JEPI, NUSI)
- REITs where depreciation exceeds cash flow
- MLPs (Master Limited Partnerships)
- Bond funds selling assets at loss or returning matured principal

## Strategic Considerations

- Best used in taxable accounts for tax bracket management
- No benefit in Traditional IRA/401(k) or Roth IRA
- Requires careful adjusted cost basis tracking
- Reinvestment compounds without tax drag
- Tax-efficient income in retirement
- Pitfalls: basis tracking failure, unrealized loss, and mischaracterization by fund companies

## Decision Framework

Five-step evaluation checklist for assessing a fund's ROC component:
1. Check distribution breakdowns
2. Track adjusted cost basis
3. Understand account-type implications
4. Evaluate true income vs headline yield
5. Assess tax deferral benefit in your specific situation
