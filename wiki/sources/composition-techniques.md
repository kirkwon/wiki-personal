# Composition Techniques

A comprehensive guide to photography composition covering practical techniques including the rule of thirds, leading lines, framing, symmetry, depth, negative space, golden ratio, color theory, perspective, minimalism, and intentional rule-breaking. Good composition arranges elements in the frame to guide the viewer's eye, create visual appeal, and produce emotional impact.

## Key Techniques

- **Rule of Thirds**: Dividing the frame into a 3x3 grid and placing key elements at intersections for balance and natural tension.
- **Leading Lines**: Using straight, curved, diagonal, or converging lines to guide the eye toward the main subject.
- **Framing**: Creating a "frame" around the subject using natural, architectural, environmental, or light-based elements.
- **Symmetry and Patterns**: Employing mirrored elements or repeating patterns for visual harmony, with deliberate breaks for interest.
- **Depth and Layers**: Using foreground, middle ground, and background layers to create a three-dimensional feel in a two-dimensional medium.
- **Negative Space**: Leveraging empty space around the subject to emphasize it, create breathing room, and add impact.
- **Golden Ratio**: Applying the mathematical 1:1.618 ratio (via Fibonacci spiral, golden triangle, or golden ratio grid) for more complex placement.
- **Color Theory in Photography**: Utilizing color harmony (monochromatic, analogous, complementary, split complementary, triadic) and temperature contrasts for visual interest.
- **Perspective**: Changing camera position (eye level, low angle, high angle, bird's eye, worm's eye) to alter the feel of an image.
- **Minimalism**: Simplifying composition to essential elements by removing distractions and using clean lines.
- **Breaking the Rules**: Intentionally breaking composition guidelines when the subject or effect demands it.

## Practice

The source emphasizes daily and weekly practice exercises, analyzing composition in both viewing and creating photos, and studying master photographers. Rules should be learned first, then deliberately broken for creative effect.
